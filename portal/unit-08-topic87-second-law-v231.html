Second Law of Thermodynamics

Energy is conserved, but not every energy transfer that satisfies conservation is physically possible. The Second Law specifies the limits on cyclic heat engines and on spontaneous heat flow.

8.7 · What does the Second Law add?

First Law · Energy balance

Energy cannot simply appear or disappear. For the sign convention used in Unit 8, Q = ΔU + Wby.

For a complete cycle the working substance returns to its initial state: ΔUcycle = 0.

Second Law · Direction and limits

The First Law does not specify which energy transfers can occur naturally or the limitations on producing useful work from a thermal source.

The Second Law explains why a cyclic heat engine needs more than one thermal reservoir, and why spontaneous heat flow has a preferred direction.

Important: The First Law is necessary, but conservation of energy alone cannot prove a proposed heat engine or refrigerator possible. The Second Law imposes additional restrictions.

Grounded in original NBF printed p.142 (start of Section 8.7). This comparison is an independently written explanation, not source page text.

Kelvin–Planck statement · The limit on cyclic heat engines

A device completing a thermodynamic cycle cannot produce net work by drawing heat from only one thermal reservoir as its sole effect. A continuously operating heat engine must transfer some energy to a colder sink.

Wnet,out = QH − QCPositive magnitudes: QH = heat supplied from hot reservoir; QC = heat rejected to cold sink.

Since ΔUcycle = 0, the energy balance is satisfied. However, a proposed cyclic engine that takes heat from one reservoir and delivers exactly the same amount as work, with no other effect, is ruled out by the Second Law.

Do not overgeneralize: A finite amount of heat can be converted to work in other processes. Kelvin–Planck rules out complete conversion as the sole effect of a cyclic engine, not every individual heat-to-work transfer.

Kelvin statement introduced on original NBF printed p.142; the cycle balance follows original Topic 8.5 and First Law.

Clausius statement · Heat does not spontaneously travel cold → hot

Heat cannot flow from a colder body to a hotter body as the sole result of a process. Heat naturally passes from hot to cold when the bodies exchange energy because of a temperature difference.

Moving energy from a colder region to a hotter one is possible when an external input (for example, work in a refrigerator) produces an additional effect. Therefore the statement is not that cold-to-hot heat transfer is absolutely impossible.

Example: A refrigerator removes heat from the cold interior, receives electrical work, and rejects energy to the warmer room. This does not violate the Clausius statement because it is not a heat-transfer process with no other effect.

Original NBF printed p.142, Clausius statement and example-level heat-flow discussion continuing onto p.143; refrigerator explanation is an independent teaching illustration.

Consistent with the Second Law

Heat naturally flows from a hotter pan to cooler surroundings when a temperature difference exists. That is the expected spontaneous direction.

Five concept checks · Reveal reasoning

1. How do the First Law and Second Law answer different questions?
The First Law enforces energy conservation. The Second Law additionally places restrictions on the direction of heat transfer and on the output of cyclic heat engines.
2. Can a cyclic heat engine take 600 J from only one heat reservoir, deliver 600 J of net work, and change nothing else?
No. Such a single-reservoir cyclic device would violate the Kelvin–Planck statement, even though its proposed energy balance is arithmetically correct.
3. Does a refrigerator violate the Clausius statement by moving heat from colder to hotter surroundings?
No. It requires additional external work and therefore does not transfer heat from cold to hot as the sole effect.
4. In a cyclic engine QH = 900 J and QC = 500 J. Find net work.
Wnet,out = QH − QC = 900 J − 500 J = 400 J. This is an energy-balance example; the Second Law also requires that proposed engines meet the physical restrictions described above.
5. Is every individual conversion of heat energy into work prohibited?
No. The Kelvin–Planck restriction concerns a cyclic device with a single thermal reservoir and complete conversion as the only effect. It does not prohibit all heat-to-work processes.
