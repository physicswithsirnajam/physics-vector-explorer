Weightlessness & Artificial Gravity Laboratory

Compare apparent weightlessness during orbital free fall with the gravity-like sensation of standing at the outer rim of a rotating station. Adjust radius and rpm to investigate how much supporting force a rotating floor would provide.

Teaching model only: Orbital motion is a qualitative free-fall illustration, not to spatial scale. The rotating habitat is an ideal rigid ring with no external gravitational field. Rotation animation is deliberately time-scaled for projector visibility; numerical values reflect the selected actual rpm. This does not predict station structural safety, human comfort, Coriolis effects or orbital trajectories, and it saves no student data.
P-11-B-16 · SUMMATIVE

1. Astronaut and satellite: why do they float?

Gravity does not vanish in an orbit. In the simplified circular-orbit model, Earth pulls both the satellite and the astronaut inward while they fall together around Earth.

Gravitational force: Present and directed toward Earth's centre.
Apparent weight: Approximately zero for a freely floating astronaut, because the normal support force from the cabin floor is approximately zero.

This is free fall, not a cancellation of gravity by a real outward force. A floating astronaut is still being accelerated by gravity.

Illustration only; relative sizes, orbit altitude and orbital period are not physical predictions.

2. Spin a ring to create a gravity-like sensation

The station floor provides a real inward normal force on a co-rotating person; the person experiences a weight-like sensation toward the outer rim. No extra real outward force is added in an inertial frame.

NBF EXAMPLE 4.4 AND ASSIGNMENT 4.4

3. Check the textbook calculations

Example 4.4 — radius 1500 m

Earth-like target 9.8 m/s²: ω ≈ 0.0808 rad/s, T ≈ 77.73 s, f ≈ 0.013 Hz, rpm ≈ 0.772. The textbook rounds ω to 0.08 rad/s.

Assignment 4.4 — 20 rpm, 8 m diameter

The radius is 4 m; ω ≈ 2.094 rad/s. The artificial acceleration is ≈ 17.55 m/s², or 1.79 g. This is a mathematical exercise, not an engineering recommendation.
