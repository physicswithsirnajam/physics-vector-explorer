Centripetal Acceleration & Centripetal Force

A body travelling around a circle can have constant speed without constant velocity. Discover why its acceleration points inward and which real forces provide that acceleration in different situations.

Assessment basis: The user-supplied FBISE HSSC-I Physics Assessment Framework identifies P-11-B-10, P-11-B-14 and P-11-B-15 as summative. This guide adapts NBF Physics XI, Unit 4 §§4.2–4.2.1 (printed pp.59–64) and Example 4.2. Applications involving detailed satellite weightlessness, artificial gravity, torque, moment of inertia and angular momentum will follow. P-11-B-10 · SUMMATIVE

1. Why does a body accelerate while travelling at constant speed?

In uniform circular motion, the speed |v| is constant, but the velocity vector changes direction continuously. Acceleration is the rate of change of the velocity vector, not merely its magnitude.

Consider two nearby points on a circular path. Their velocity vectors point along the respective tangents. Subtracting them gives a change in velocity that points toward the circle's centre in the limiting, very short time interval.

Magnitude of centripetal acceleration: ac = v²/r = rω²
Direction: Centripetal acceleration is always radially toward the centre, perpendicular to the instantaneous tangential velocity. For a circle at constant speed, tangential acceleration is zero but centripetal acceleration is not (when v ≠ 0).

In vector form the radial acceleration can be written as ac = −(v²/r) r̂, if r̂ is the unit vector pointing outward. The negative sign specifies the inward direction; it does not mean the acceleration magnitude is negative.

Concept check: If an object on a string is released, which way does it initially travel?

Immediately after release, it moves along the tangent at the release point, because the inward tension force is no longer provided. It does not travel straight outward along the radius.

P-11-B-14 · SUMMATIVE

2. What is centripetal force?

By Newton's second law, the body needs an inward net force to follow a circular trajectory. The term centripetal force describes the resultant radial force; it is not a new or additional fundamental force acting on top of tension, friction, gravity or normal force.

Magnitude: Fc = m ac = mv²/r = mrω²

Here m is mass in kilograms, v is tangential speed in m/s, r is radius in metres and Fc is in newtons. These expressions apply to motion along a circle; the interactive lab uses constant speed so the tangential net force is zero.

Very important: Doubling speed at fixed m and r multiplies the required inward force by 4; doubling the radius at fixed m and v reduces it to one-half; doubling mass at fixed v and r doubles the force.
Worked practice: A 2.0 kg object moves at 6 m/s on a circle of radius 3 m. Find ac and Fc.

ac = v²/r = 6²/3 = 12 m/s² inward. Fc = m ac = 2.0 ×12 = 24 N inward.

NBF §§4.2–4.2.1, equations (4.18–4.21), printed pp.60–61.

P-11-B-15 · SUMMATIVE

3. Which actual force provides the inward result?

SituationWhat supplies the required net inward force?
Car turning on a level, unbanked roadStatic friction between tyres and road, provided the tyres do not slip.
Car following a properly banked, friction-free curveThe horizontal component of the normal reaction toward the turn centre.
Object moving in a circle on a stringTension supplies the radial force where it is the only radial force.
Planet or satellite following a circular orbitGravitational attraction supplies the inward force. Detailed orbital and weightlessness physics comes later.

The specific real force depends on the circumstances; the inward resultant must meet Fc = mv²/r. Do not add an extra separate "centripetal force" to the force diagram.

Friction-free banked road

For a bank angle θ (road surface measured from horizontal), the normal reaction N is tilted inward. Under the ideal conditions of no friction and no vertical acceleration:

N cosθ = mg    |    N sinθ = mv²/r tanθ = v²/(rg)

Mass cancels. This is the design angle for a particular speed under the textbook's friction-free assumption. It does not mean that every speed is safe on a banked curve without friction.

Worked practice: Find the ideal bank angle for v = 12 m/s and r = 50 m (g = 9.8 m/s²).

tanθ = 12²/(50×9.8) = 144/490 ≈ 0.2939. Therefore θ = arctan(0.2939) ≈ 16.4°.

String, gravity and a centrifuge

The textbook uses a string-tension example and gravity keeping a planet or satellite in orbit. It also describes how a centrifuge separates suspended material: in the laboratory frame, the liquid is forced into curved motion while particles with insufficient inward force move relative to it. Avoid confusing the outward-looking separation with an extra real outward force in an inertial force diagram.

4. Worked example — radius of a car's turn

A vehicle of mass 856 kg moves around a curve at 12.0 m/s. The necessary centripetal force is 7250 N. Calculate the radius.

  1. Write the centripetal-force relation: Fc = mv²/r.
  2. Rearrange for r: r = mv²/Fc.
  3. Substitute: r = (856 kg)(12.0 m/s)² / (7250 N) = 123264/7250 m.
  4. Calculate: r ≈ 17.0 m, or approximately 17 m as shown in the textbook.
