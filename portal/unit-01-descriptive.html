Section I — Short-response practice

S1: Express common derived units in terms of SI base units

Related to short Exercise 1.2

P-11-A-02
Model answer

Force: F = ma → kg m s−2. Work: W = Fd → kg m2 s−2. Power: P = W/t → kg m2 s−3. Pressure: force/area → kg m−1 s−2. Electric charge: Q = It → A s. Each is a combination of SI base units, not a new base unit.

S2: Why does instrument resolution matter?

Related to short Exercise 1.3

P-11-A-05
Model answer

The least count (resolution) is the smallest instrument step or discernible change. Finer resolution generally enables more detailed readings and can improve repeatability. But resolution alone does not guarantee accuracy: systematic calibration errors, parallax and the measurement technique can still shift the reading.

S3: Why take repeated measurements?

Related to short Exercise 1.4

P-11-A-05 & P-11-A-07
Model answer

Repeated observations reveal random variation, permit a mean and a spread to be estimated, and help identify outliers or unstable methods. They do not automatically remove a systematic offset; good calibration and procedure are still needed.

S4: Differentiate accuracy and precision

Related to short Exercise 1.5

P-11-A-05
Model answer

Accuracy measures closeness to a true or accepted reference. Precision describes closeness among repeated measurements. A poorly calibrated instrument can give readings that are highly precise but consistently far from the reference. For example, a mass of 50.0 g repeatedly recorded as 52.1, 52.0 and 52.1 g is precise but inaccurate.

S5: Explain the principle of dimensional homogeneity

Related to short Exercise 1.6

P-11-A-03
Model answer

Only quantities with the same dimensions may be added or subtracted in a physical equation. Thus, the left and right sides—and every additive term—must have matching dimensions. For s = ut + ½at², each term has dimension [L]. Dimensional agreement is necessary, but not sufficient, to establish physical correctness.

S6: Combine five time readings and report uncertainty

Related to short Exercise 1.7; numerical details paraphrased

P-11-A-06 & P-11-A-07
Model answer

With times 6.2, 6.0, 6.4, 6.1 and 5.8 s, the mean is (6.2 + 6.0 + 6.4 + 6.1 + 5.8)/5 = 6.10 s. The absolute deviations from 6.10 s are 0.1, 0.1, 0.3, 0.0, 0.3 s; their mean is 0.16 s. A classroom mean-absolute-deviation report is therefore about (6.1 ± 0.2) s after sensible rounding. This spread is one possible uncertainty estimate; the choice of reporting convention must be stated.

S7: Find the dimensions of Planck's constant

Related to short Exercise 1.8

P-11-A-02
Model answer

For photon energy E = hf, rearrange h = E/f. Since [E] = [M L² T−2] and [f] = [T−1], we obtain [h] = [M L² T−1]. Its SI unit is J s = kg m² s−1.

S8: Explain why all measurements have uncertainty

Related to short Exercise 1.9

P-11-A-07
Model answer

Measurements are limited by instrument resolution, calibration, observer response and changing conditions. Even repeatable readings cannot establish the exact true value with unlimited certainty. We therefore report a best estimate and a defensible uncertainty interval, for example (3.06 ± 0.02) mm.

S9: Compare random and systematic error

Original reinforcement question

P-11-A-05 & P-11-A-07
Model answer

Random error makes successive readings fluctuate unpredictably around a value; repeats help quantify this spread. Systematic error consistently biases readings in one direction; recalibration or correcting the technique is generally needed. Both may occur together.

S10: Why can't dimensions prove a complete equation?

Original reinforcement question

P-11-A-03 & P-11-A-04
Model answer

Dimensions compare physical types and exponent patterns, so they can rule out many incorrect equations but cannot establish dimensionless coefficients such as ½ and 2π, or determine the exact functional form of trigonometric or exponential relationships. Experimental or theoretical physics must supply those details.

Section II — Comprehensive-response practice

E1: Define measurement uncertainty and explain its origin

Based on comprehensive Exercise 1.1

P-11-A-07

Meaning

Uncertainty is a quantified indication of the range of values reasonably compatible with a measured result. It is different from a mistake or from knowing the exact error, which usually cannot be measured directly.

Why it occurs

No measuring instrument has unlimited resolution; calibration may be imperfect; environmental conditions can vary; human reaction time and reading technique can contribute.

Good reporting

Write a value and absolute uncertainty together with a unit: x = (25.4 ± 0.2) cm. Use consistent significant figures, and explain how the interval was estimated.

Reduction and limitations

Use an appropriate calibrated instrument, repeated readings and careful procedures. These can reduce or characterize some uncertainties but cannot eliminate all sources.

E2: Explain uncertainty propagation in final results

Based on comprehensive Exercise 1.2

P-11-A-06

Addition/subtraction

For z = x ± y, the simple conservative method adds absolute uncertainties: Δz = Δx + Δy. Example: (15.0 ± 0.2) cm − (4.0 ± 0.1) cm = (11.0 ± 0.3) cm.

Multiplication/division

For z = xy or x/y, approximately add fractional uncertainties: Δz/|z| ≈ Δx/|x| + Δy/|y|. This is the simple chapter rule, not a full covariance analysis.

Powers

For z = xn and small relative uncertainty, Δz/|z| ≈ |n| Δx/|x|. Hence an area proportional to r² has roughly twice the radius percentage uncertainty.

Final answer

Keep adequate digits during working; calculate nominal value and uncertainty; round the uncertainty and value consistently, and include units.

E3: Dimensions of a physical quantity: purpose and applications

Based on comprehensive Exercise 1.3

P-11-A-02, A-03 & A-04

Meaning

Dimensions describe the powers of the base physical quantities in a derived quantity, written with symbols such as M, L and T. For velocity, [v] = [L T−1].

Checking equations

Inspect dimensional homogeneity; for s = ut + ½at², all terms have [L]. An expression adding [L] and [L T−1] is invalid.

Deriving relations

Assume an unknown relationship as a product of powers of relevant variables, substitute dimensions and equate exponents. For a small-angle pendulum, assuming T ∝ maℓbgc gives a = 0, b = ½ and c = −½, hence T ∝ √(ℓ/g).

Limits

Dimensions alone do not supply numerical coefficients or complex functional forms; a homogeneous equation is not necessarily physically correct.

E4: Estimation: explain the method with a practical example

Based on comprehensive Exercise 1.4

P-11-A-01 · Formative

Meaning

An estimate is a reasoned approximation built from prior knowledge and a realistic reference, not a random guess.

Method

Specify the desired quantity and unit; choose a reference; scale it to the situation; report assumptions; check plausibility.

Example

A four-storey building with an assumed 3 m per storey has an estimated height 4 × 3 = 12 m, excluding roof structures.

Use and assessment status

Estimates help check calculations and plan experiments, but P-11-A-01 is classified as formative in the supplied FBISE framework and is not a summative theory-paper SLO.

Teacher / student answer-writing checklist

  • State the relevant principle and distinguish definitions accurately.
  • For quantities, write an equation and meaningful SI units.
  • For dimensional analysis, equate the powers of each base dimension explicitly.
  • For uncertainty calculations, report the chosen propagation rule and assumptions.
  • For examples, show steps in a logical order rather than providing only the final result.
  • Remember that a formative outcome may be taught and checked in class without being a summative final-exam outcome.
