Air Resistance, Momentum & Collisions

Textbook-based study notes for qualitative drag effects and one-dimensional collision analysis, with clear attention to momentum conservation, kinetic energy and the FBISE learning-outcome classification.

P-11-B-06 · Summative

2. Effect of air resistance on projectiles

In an ideal projectile model, there is no horizontal acceleration and the vertical acceleration is g downward. Actual air resistance acts opposite the instantaneous velocity, changes both component motions and generally shortens the range and maximum height relative to otherwise identical no-drag conditions.

FeatureNo air resistanceWith air resistance
Horizontal velocityRemains constantGenerally decreases while the projectile travels forward
Vertical motionAcceleration always −g if upward is positiveDrag acts downward on ascent and upward on descent; acceleration depends on motion
Maximum heightH = u²sin²θ/(2g) for launch from a level surfaceGenerally smaller for the same launch conditions
RangeR = u²sin(2θ)/g for same-height landingGenerally shorter; the ideal formula does not apply
TrajectoryIdeal parabolaNot an exact parabola; depends on drag conditions
Explain why the horizontal velocity changes (student self-check)

The resistive force is opposite the direction of movement. Therefore the horizontal component of net force is no longer zero when the projectile moves forward, so horizontal velocity is not constant.

Do complementary launch angles necessarily have equal ranges with air drag?

No. The equal-range property R(θ)=R(90°−θ) follows from the ideal no-drag, equal-launch-and-landing-height formula. Real drag changes the trajectory and does not generally preserve that symmetry.

Source: NBF Physics XI Figure 3.6(b), printed page 42. Our companion interactive diagram is qualitative; it does not calculate drag numerically.

P-11-B-11 · Formative

3. The law of conservation of momentum

Momentum is a vector: p⃗ = m v⃗. For an isolated system with no external resultant impulse, total momentum before and after an interaction is the same.

Σp⃗ before = Σp⃗ after For a straight-line two-body interaction: m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂

A signed velocity convention is essential. Motion in the negative direction contributes negative momentum. The textbook discusses recoil, explosions, ball-and-bat interactions and rocket thrust to explain conservation of momentum.

Important teaching distinction: P-11-B-11 is classified formative by the FBISE framework. Learn and practise conservation of momentum; it is also a foundation for the summative collision-analysis SLOs below.

P-11-B-12 · Summative

4. Elastic collisions

For an elastic collision in an isolated system, both total momentum and total kinetic energy are conserved.

m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ ½m₁u₁² + ½m₂u₂² = ½m₁v₁² + ½m₂v₂²

For an ideal head-on elastic collision in one dimension where object 1 approaches object 2, the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation:

u₁ − u₂ = v₂ − v₁

This follows from combining the momentum and kinetic-energy equations for the elastic interaction (where the relative speeds are nonzero).

v₁ = [(m₁−m₂)u₁ + 2m₂u₂] / (m₁+m₂) v₂ = [2m₁u₁ + (m₂−m₁)u₂] / (m₁+m₂)

Textbook Example 3.7 — printed pages 48–49

A 5 kg body travels at 10 m/s and collides with a 10 kg body moving in the same direction at 5 m/s. If the collision is one-dimensional and elastic, find their final velocities.

Reveal the complete calculation

Given: m₁ = 5 kg, m₂ = 10 kg, u₁ = 10 m/s, u₂ = 5 m/s.

v₁ = [(5−10)(10) + 2(10)(5)] / (5+10) = (−50+100)/15 = 3.33 m/s.

v₂ = [2(5)(10) + (10−5)(5)] / (5+10) = (100+25)/15 = 8.33 m/s.

Momentum check: Before = 5(10)+10(5)=100 kg·m/s; after = 5(3.333…)+10(8.333…)=100 kg·m/s.

Kinetic energy check: Before = ½(5)(10²)+½(10)(5²)=375 J; after = 375 J (before rounding the final velocities).

Relative speed check: Approach = 10−5=5 m/s; separation = 8.333…−3.333…=5 m/s.

The two bodies remain distinct after the elastic interaction; their velocities are not necessarily equal. Rounding displayed final velocities can create tiny apparent differences in momentum or energy checks.

P-11-B-13 · Summative

5. Inelastic collisions and kinetic-energy change

Momentum remains conserved for an isolated system during an inelastic collision, but total kinetic energy is not generally conserved. Some kinetic energy is transformed into deformation, thermal energy, sound or other energy forms.

Perfectly inelastic is a special case: the bodies stick together and move with the same final velocity. This extra instructional model is useful for comparing energy before and after the collision.

v₁ = v₂ = v = (m₁u₁+m₂u₂)/(m₁+m₂)

Additional worked illustration — bodies stick together

Use the same initial values as textbook Example 3.7 (m₁=5 kg, m₂=10 kg, u₁=10 m/s, u₂=5 m/s), but now suppose they stick together. This is not the textbook's original elastic Example 3.7.

Show momentum and energy calculations

Common final speed: v = [5(10)+10(5)]/(5+10) = 100/15 = 6.67 m/s.

Momentum: 100 kg·m/s before and after.

Kinetic energy before: ½(5)(10²)+½(10)(5²) = 375 J.

Kinetic energy after: ½(15)(100/15)² = 333.33 J.

Decrease: 375−333.33 = 41.67 J. Energy is transformed into other forms, not destroyed.

Why is total momentum conserved even when kinetic energy decreases?

Momentum conservation follows from negligible net external impulse on the isolated system. Kinetic energy can be transferred into deformation, heat or sound during an inelastic interaction, so it need not be conserved as kinetic energy even though total energy remains conserved.

Source: NBF Physics XI pp. 44–49, sections 3.3–3.4. The one-dimensional stick-together example is separately identified as an instructional extension.

6. Classroom / examination preparation

  • Explain how resistance affects the horizontal and vertical components of a projectile qualitatively (summative B-06).
  • For elastic collisions, calculate and verify final velocities, momentum, kinetic energy and relative speed (summative B-12).
  • Contrast an elastic collision with an inelastic one to explain kinetic-energy change (summative B-13).
  • Practise momentum applications as valuable formative learning (B-11), without mislabelling this individual SLO as summative theory.
