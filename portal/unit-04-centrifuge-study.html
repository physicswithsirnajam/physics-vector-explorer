How Does a Centrifuge Separate Materials?

A centrifuge spins a sample rapidly about an axis. By connecting Newton's first law with the inward force required for circular motion, we can explain why different components of a mixture move relative to the spinning tube.

FBISE assessment: P-11-B-21 — Summative (Application): justify how a centrifuge separates materials using centripetal force. NBF Grade XI Physics Unit 4, printed p.63, Figure 4.11 (centrifuge). Related questions: MCQ 4.1 on p.74, short-response 4.2 on p.75 and comprehensive question 4.6 on p.76. This guide supports source-grounded teaching; it is not an official FBISE marking scheme.
P-11-B-21 · SUMMATIVE

1. The physical principle

For an object to travel around a circular path of radius r at speed v, the net inward (centripetal) force must be sufficient to continuously change the direction of its velocity. For constant-speed circular motion:

ac = v²/r = ω²r    |    Fc = mac = mω²r

The test tube and liquid are set into rotation. The rotating container and liquid provide the forces needed for curved motion. Particles in the liquid do not all respond in the same way: differences in density, inertia, buoyancy and fluid resistance produce relative movement within the spinning sample. In a typical suspension, denser particles accumulate toward the outer end (the bottom of the tube), while a less dense liquid remains closer to the axis.

Key reasoning: Without the required inward force to follow the container's circular path, a particle tends to continue according to its existing motion (Newton's first law). Relative to the spinning tube, this can produce outward separation. In a frame rotating with the sample, the same relative behaviour is commonly described using an apparent centrifugal effect.

The real inward resultant and the rotating-frame apparent outward effect are descriptions using different reference frames. Do not add an independent real outward centrifugal force to the inertial-frame free-body diagram.

FOLLOW THE PROCESS

2. From a mixture to separated material

  1. Load the mixture: A liquid containing suspended material is placed inside a test tube or suitable rotating container.
  2. Spin the rotor: The motor drives the sample around the axis. The tube's outer end is farther from the centre and is its bottom during outward sedimentation.
  3. Different responses: The different material components experience different effective settling behaviour as they move relative to the rotating liquid. Dense suspended material can collect toward the outer end.
  4. Collect the fractions: After controlled spinning and stopping, the more concentrated fraction can be separated from the less concentrated liquid. The degree of separation depends on the sample and equipment; the model does not predict a settling time.

The textbook says small, denser particles settle at the bottom of the tube during centrifugation. This qualitative account is the required classroom idea, not a general formula for sedimentation speed.

LINK BACK TO UNIT 4 CENTRIPETAL FORCE

4. What happens when the machine spins faster?

For a particle kept in co-rotation at the same radius, the inward acceleration required is ac = ω²r. Rotation rate in revolutions per minute (rpm) converts to radians per second as:

ω = rpm × 2π/60

At a fixed radius, doubling rpm quadruples the centripetal acceleration demand. At fixed rpm, doubling the radius doubles this demand. These equations alone cannot determine how quickly real particles settle or how efficiently a machine separates a specific material.

Practice: A spinning rotor operates at 600 rpm, radius 0.10 m. Calculate the inward acceleration needed for a sample to co-rotate.

ω = 600×2π/60 = 20π ≈ 62.83 rad/s. Therefore ac = ω²r = (62.83)²(0.10) ≈ 394.8 m/s². This is about 40.3 times g using g=9.8 m/s². The result describes the required inward circular acceleration, not a prediction of the sample's settling time.

EXAM-ORIENTED CONCEPT CHECKS

5. Can you explain it independently?

Question 1 — What does the term “centrifugation” mean in the textbook's MCQ exercise?

It means separation through spinning. NBF Unit 4 exercise MCQ 1, printed p.74.

Question 2 — Why can water escape through the holes of a spinning washing-machine drum?

The rotating drum keeps clothing following a circular path, but water can leave through openings when it is not held by sufficient inward constraint. Relative to the drum it moves outward; once free, its motion initially follows its instantaneous velocity. NBF short-response question 4.2, p.75.

Question 3 — Justify the use of a centrifuge to separate materials using centripetal force.

Suggested three-point response: (1) A centrifuge spins the mixture rapidly and requires inward centripetal force for co-rotation. (2) Components of different density/inertia interact differently with the rotating liquid and container; denser suspended particles tend to settle toward the tube's outer end. (3) The separated fractions can then be collected, as in a laboratory centrifuge or milk cream separator. Describe the apparent outward effect only as a rotating-reference-frame explanation.
