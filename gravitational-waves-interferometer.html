What to notice

01 · Accelerating masses
Even with constant orbital speed, the velocity direction changes, so each orbiting body is accelerating.
02 · Ripples in spacetime
A changing mass distribution can radiate gravitational waves. A symmetric isolated body moving uniformly does not produce the same radiation.
03 · Frequency
For the leading radiation from a circular binary, gravitational-wave frequency is twice the orbital frequency.
NBF pp. 179–180: gravitational waves are generated by accelerating masses; distant massive systems are suitable detection sources.

Design your interferometer

For an ideal alignment, the two perpendicular arms vary oppositely as a plus-polarized wave passes.

Lₓ = L(1 + h/2)  ;  Lᵧ = L(1 − h/2)
ΔL = Lₓ − Lᵧ = Lh
Optical path difference = 2ΔL
Δφ = (4π/λ)ΔL
These relations are an idealized mathematical enrichment, beyond the FBISE theory requirement for D-18. NBF p. 179 illustrates perpendicular 4-km interferometer arms.

Classroom facilitation

20-minute projector lesson

Engage (3 min)
Ask: “Do gravitational waves mean gravitational force switches off and on?” Let students explain their prediction.
Explore (5 min)
Open Binary Source. Increase orbital frequency. Ask why orbiting motion counts as acceleration, even if speed magnitude is fixed.
Explain (5 min)
Open Space Distortion and freeze the wave at 90° then 270°. Explain complementary stretch/compression using freely falling test masses.
Elaborate (5 min)
Open Interferometer. Keep the arm length at 4 km. Read the attometre-sized difference aloud. Emphasize the exaggerated drawing.
Evaluate (2 min)
Set two concept MCQs as an exit check; students write one accurate statement and one misconception to avoid.

Model limitations
The source animation is schematic; the strain graphic is exaggerated. The interferometer uses a simplified plus-polarization geometry and omits cavities, control loops, detector noise, antenna response and full general relativity.
