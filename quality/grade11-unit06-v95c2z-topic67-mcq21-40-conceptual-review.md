## Key independent scientific reasoning

**Q21–23, Q25, Q27, Q39–40 — core B43 conceptual Understanding:** Zero viscosity is not zero density, mass or speed. A superfluid may move, and the lack of ordinary viscous losses can permit persistent circulation. 'Without loss of kinetic energy' is shorthand for lack of ordinary viscous damping, not the universal inability to exchange energy or produce arbitrary energy. Original answer letters A/A/A/C/A/B/B have sound textbook-model justifications. Q39's ideal persistence is directly relevant to the official B43 example.

**Q24 — causal wording versus source support:** The original B states the low-temperature superfluid state, illustrated by flow over walls. It is the best offered answer but does not give an independent mechanism for the requested 'most direct cause.' The NBF printed page introduces creep as a superfluid implication, rather than a fully derived microscopic process. A teacher should revise the stem to ask what the superfluid *does*, or keep the original practice and add an improved separate item. No source answer letter is silently changed.

**Q26, Q31–34 — rotating vessel/quantization:** Printed p.106 says superfluid may remain stationary while a container begins slow rotation, then vortices begin to nucleate above a critical angular speed; additional quantized vortices may form at greater speeds. Each vortex circulation has discrete allowed strengths, unlike unrestricted classical continuous circulation in ordinary fluids. The official B43's direct explicit example is **persistent vortices**, so detailed quantization thresholds and vocabulary are useful **textbook enrichment**, not automatically individually summative-certified. Q32 requires textbook's ideal subcritical assumptions; it does not claim any real sample is immune to normal-fluid coupling and boundary disturbances.

**Q28, Q29–30, Q37 — helium state/pressure graph:** Original Fig6.17 indicates separate normal liquid/superfluid and other phases as pressure and temperature vary, supporting Q28 A and Q37 D. Q28 explicitly names **Figure6.17** but the source's `referenceFigureId` is absent. Q37 is **self-contained as phrased**—no missing figure is asserted there. Cooling toward absolute zero *can* produce a superfluid state for helium with appropriate pressure/isotope conditions (Q29 C); the helium-3/helium-4 benchmark thresholds differ (Q30 C) and are not universal pressure-independent numbers.

**Q35–36 — quantum and superconducting contexts:** The NBF page attributes superfluidity to quantum behavior and mentions superconducting electrons in a solid; Q35 A and Q36 C are best in their source context. Superfluidity concerns fluid-flow behavior while conventional superconductivity concerns electronic transport; do not imply all superconductors are always solids or that the two phenomena are interchangeable. Q36 is enrichment beyond B43's exact implications.

**Q38 — textbook gas expansion-cooling sequence:** The printed original describes compressing helium, then letting it expand through a narrow nozzle and cool. Source A is an accurate *attributed textbook sequence*, but whether any real helium gas cools upon expansion depends on starting thermodynamic state and expansion method (throttling or work-producing), so do not promote a universal physics claim without conditions. This is refrigeration enrichment rather than mandatory B43 zero-viscosity/creep/vortex content.
