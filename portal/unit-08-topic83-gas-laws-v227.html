8.3 · Compare the three gas laws

8.3.1 · Boyle's Law

Keep temperature and amount of gas constant. Pressure is inversely proportional to volume: compressing a gas raises its pressure.

p ∝ 1/VpV = constant
p1V1 = p2V2

As volume doubles, pressure halves, provided temperature and gas amount remain unchanged.

NBF printed p.134; Figure 8.7 is not reproduced.

8.3.2 · Charles's Law

Keep pressure and amount of gas constant. Gas volume increases in direct proportion to its absolute temperature.

V ∝ TV/T = constant
V1/T1 = V2/T2

Use kelvin, never Celsius values directly in the temperature ratios. A rise from 300 K to 600 K doubles the ideal-gas volume at constant pressure.

NBF printed pp.134–135; Figure 8.8 is not reproduced.

8.3.3 · Gay-Lussac's Law

Keep volume and amount of gas constant. Gas pressure increases in direct proportion to its absolute temperature.

p ∝ Tp/T = constant
p1/T1 = p2/T2

In a rigid vessel, increasing temperature increases ideal-gas pressure if the amount of gas is unchanged.

NBF printed p.135; Figure 8.9 is not reproduced.

Always use absolute temperature: T(K) ≈ t(°C) + 273.15. The textbook's Charles-law line, when extrapolated, reaches zero volume near −273 °C. That is a mathematical ideal-gas extrapolation, not a physical claim that a real gas can be cooled continuously to zero volume.

Interactive gas-law investigations · Unscored

Choose an investigation and vary one independent quantity while holding the stated variables constant. These are original practice scenarios, not original numbered textbook examples or assignments. Inputs are calculated in the browser only.

A · Predict final pressure at constant T

At constant temperature, p₂ = (200 × 10)/25 = 80 kPa.

p₂ = p₁V₁/V₂; 200 kPa × 10 L / 25 L = 80 kPa. Consistent volume units cancel.

B · Predict volume at constant p

At constant pressure, V₂ = (18 × 360)/270 = 24 L.

V₂ = V₁T₂/T₁. Both temperatures must be expressed in kelvin.

C · Predict pressure at constant V

At constant volume, p₂ = (100 × 450)/300 = 150 kPa.

p₂ = p₁T₂/T₁. This is an ideal-gas prediction for a fixed gas amount.

Units and limitations: These calculators use positive absolute gas pressures (kPa), positive volumes (L), and positive temperatures (K). They do not model gas leaks, changing gas amount, liquefaction, strong real-gas effects or non-constant constraints.

Four conceptual self-checks · No scores

1. A fixed gas sample occupies 6 L at 100 kPa. At constant temperature, what pressure corresponds to 3 L?
By Boyle's law, p₂ = (100 kPa)(6 L)/(3 L) = 200 kPa. Halving volume doubles pressure.
2. Why is 30 °C not inserted directly into Charles's law as T = 30?
Because gas-law temperature ratios require an absolute scale. 30 °C is approximately 303.15 K, not 30 K.
3. A closed rigid vessel is warmed from 300 K to 450 K. What happens to pressure in the ideal model?
At fixed volume and gas amount, p₂/p₁ = 450/300 = 1.5. The absolute pressure rises by 50%.
4. What is the shape of a pressure–volume graph at constant temperature, and what must stay fixed?
Pressure decreases hyperbolically as volume increases, with pV constant. Both the absolute temperature and amount of gas stay fixed.
