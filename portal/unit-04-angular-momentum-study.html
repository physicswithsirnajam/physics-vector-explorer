Moment of Inertia, Torque & Angular Momentum

Rotating objects resist changes to their rotation, just as mass measures resistance to changes in straight-line motion. Learn which quantity describes that resistance, how torque changes angular momentum, and why a skater spins faster with arms drawn inward.

Source and FBISE scope: NBF Grade XI Physics, Unit 4 printed pp.64–71 (§§4.3–4.5), including Example 4.3 on p.70. Summative theory SLOs P-11-B-18, P-11-B-19 and P-11-B-20 are addressed here. P-11-B-18 · SUMMATIVE

1. Moment of inertia about a specified axis

Moment of inertia I measures the rotational resistance to a change in angular velocity about a particular axis. Unlike ordinary mass, it depends on where the mass lies relative to that axis. More mass farther from the rotation axis means larger I.

Point mass: I = mr²    |    Rigid body: I = Σmᵢrᵢ²

The SI unit is kg m². For a body's moment of inertia, the axis and its mass distribution must be specified. The following NBF models use symmetry axes through their centres:

Body and rotation axisMoment of inertiaRelative value at same mass M and radius R
Single point mass, distance R from axisI = MR²1.00 MR²
Thin hoop, central symmetry axisI = MR²1.00 MR²
Uniform solid disc or cylinder, central symmetry axisI = ½MR²0.50 MR²
Uniform solid sphere, diameter as axisI = ⅖MR²0.40 MR²
Thin hollow spherical shell, diameter as axisI = ⅔MR²≈0.667 MR²

These coefficients are not interchangeable. For example, a uniform disc and a thin hoop of the same mass and radius have different I because the disc's material is not all located at the outer radius.

Practice: A uniform solid disc has mass 2.0 kg and radius 0.50 m. Find I about its central axis.

I = ½MR² = ½(2.0)(0.50)² = 0.25 kg m². For a thin hoop with the same mass and radius, I would instead be 0.50 kg m².

2. Angular momentum

For a particle, angular momentum relative to an origin is the vector cross product of its position and linear momentum:

L⃗ = r⃗ × p⃗    |    magnitude L = rp sinθ

Here θ is the angle between position r⃗ and linear momentum p⃗. For a particle moving tangentially to a circle, θ = 90°, so L = rp = mr²ω. For a rigid body rotating about a fixed principal axis:

L = Iω    (signed component along the chosen rotation axis)

The SI unit of angular momentum is kg m²/s (equivalent to J s). The direction follows the right-hand rule. We must distinguish angular momentum about the chosen rotation axis from unrelated motions or axes.

Practice: The solid disc above rotates at 4 rad/s. What is its axial angular momentum?

I = 0.25 kg m², so L = Iω = 0.25×4 = 1.0 kg m²/s along the chosen positive axis.

NBF §4.4 and §§4.4.1–4.4.2, printed pp.65–66, equations (4.26–4.37).

P-11-B-19 · SUMMATIVE

3. Torque and angular acceleration

Torque is the turning effect of a force relative to a chosen axis or point. Its vector expression and magnitude are:

τ⃗ = r⃗ × F⃗    |    |τ| = rF sinθ

θ is the angle between the position vector and applied force. With a force applied tangentially (θ = 90°), |τ| = rF. The SI torque unit is N m.

The net external torque changes angular momentum:

Στext = dL/dt    |    average Στext = ΔL/Δt

For a rigid body with fixed moment of inertia about the selected axis, L = Iω and therefore:

Στext = Iα    (constant I, fixed rotation axis)
Important limit: Do not apply τ = Iα without qualification when I changes during the motion. More generally, d(Iω)/dt also contains a contribution from changing I. A skater changing arm position is treated separately using conservation of angular momentum.
Practice: A body has I = 2.0 kg m² and is subjected to a +6.0 N m net axial torque. Find angular acceleration.

α = τ/I = +6.0/2.0 = +3.0 rad/s². Positive torque is defined in the chosen positive axis direction.

NBF §4.4.3 printed pp.66–67, equations (4.38–4.42); §4.5 and §4.5.1 printed pp.70–71, equations (4.44–4.50).

4. Textbook numerical — grinding wheel brought to rest

A uniform cylindrical grinding wheel has mass M = 3.6 kg, radius R = 31 cm = 0.31 m, and spins at 1150 rpm. Find the initial angular momentum and the torque needed to stop it in 7.8 s, treating the stopping torque as constant.

  1. Convert rpm to rad/s: ω₀ = 1150×(2π/60) ≈ 120.4 rad/s.
  2. Disc moment of inertia: I = ½MR² = ½(3.6)(0.31)² ≈ 0.17298 kg m².
  3. Initial angular momentum: L₀ = Iω₀ ≈ 20.83 kg m²/s.
  4. Stopping torque: τ = (Lfinal−L₀)/Δt = (0−20.83)/7.8 ≈ −2.67 N m.

The negative sign means the torque acts opposite the selected initial direction of rotation; the torque magnitude is approximately 2.67 N m. The wheel is assumed to stop at the stated time without reversing during the braking interval.

The Part 4 interactive laboratory includes the textbook values as a preset and computes the unrounded result directly.

P-11-B-20 · SUMMATIVE

5. Conservation of angular momentum

If the net external torque about the chosen axis is zero, angular momentum about that axis remains constant:

Στext = 0    ⇒    Linitial = Lfinal Iᵢωᵢ = Ifωf    (same rotation axis)

A spinning ice skater pulling their arms inward has a smaller moment of inertia, so their angular speed increases if external torque is negligible. Extending the arms has the opposite effect.

Angular momentum ≠ rotational kinetic energy. For a skater changing arm position, L can remain constant while rotational kinetic energy changes, because the skater does internal work when moving the arms.

Textbook situations

  • Ice skater: Drawing in arms reduces I and increases ω; extending arms increases I and decreases ω.
  • Diving athlete: Changing body shape during a dive changes rotational inertia and angular speed under small external torque.
  • Gyroscope: The textbook discusses gyroscopic effects and precession under an applied gravitational torque. Gyroscopic precession is not modeled by the single-axis skater slider.
  • Flywheel: A rotating flywheel stores kinetic energy and can help smooth variations in shaft speed. A flywheel is not a perpetual-motion or energy-creating device.
Practice: If a skater reduces moment of inertia from 6 to 3 kg m² and initially spins at 2 rad/s, what is the final angular speed?

Using Iᵢωᵢ = Ifωf: (6)(2) = (3)ωf, therefore ωf = 4 rad/s. Rotational kinetic energy can increase because the skater performs internal work; the conservation law applies to angular momentum, not necessarily kinetic energy.
