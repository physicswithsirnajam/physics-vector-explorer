1. Estimation of physical quantities

P-11-A-01 · Formative

Estimation is a reasoned approximation based on prior experience, familiar reference values, and simple physical relationships. It is not a random guess. Estimation is valuable when direct measurement is impractical or when we want to check whether a numerical answer is reasonable.

A practical estimation method

  1. Identify the physical quantity and its SI unit.
  2. Choose a familiar reference (for example, roughly 3 m for a storey of a building).
  3. Count or compare how many references fit the situation.
  4. Round to a sensible level of precision; state assumptions.
  5. Check whether the result is plausible.
Example — estimate a building's height. Suppose a building has four storeys and each is approximately 3 m high. Height ≈ 4 × 3 m = 12 m. The estimate depends on assumed storey height and excludes features such as rooftop structures.

This skill helps in learning and problem checking. According to the provided FBISE framework, P-11-A-01 is formative rather than a final-examination theory SLO.

2. Derived SI units and dimensions

P-11-A-02 · Summative

A base quantity is expressed using an SI base unit (such as mass in kilograms, length in metres or time in seconds). A derived quantity combines base quantities mathematically. Its SI unit can therefore be written as a product or quotient of base units.

QuantityDefining relationSI base unitsDimensions
Velocitydisplacement ÷ timem s−1[L T−1]
Accelerationchange in velocity ÷ timem s−2[L T−2]
Forcemass × accelerationkg m s−2[M L T−2]
Work / energyforce × displacementkg m2 s−2[M L2 T−2]
Powerwork ÷ timekg m2 s−3[M L2 T−3]
Pressureforce ÷ areakg m−1 s−2[M L−1 T−2]
Electric chargecurrent × timeA s[I T]

Units versus dimensions: metre and second are specific units, but [L] and [T] represent the physical types length and time. A dimensionless number, such as a numerical coefficient or a ratio of like quantities, has dimension [1].

Worked example — express the joule using base units.
W = Fd;   F = ma   ⇒   [W] = kg × (m s−2) × m = kg m2 s−2
Therefore, 1 J = 1 kg m² s⁻².

Common mistake: writing the dimension of force as “N”. Newton (N) is a unit; [M L T⁻²] is its dimension.

3. Dimensional homogeneity

P-11-A-03 · Summative

An equation is dimensionally homogeneous when every term that is added or subtracted has the same dimensions, and the dimensions of the left and right sides match. Terms of unlike dimensions cannot be added meaningfully.

Worked example — check a motion equation

s = ut + ½at2
Step 1: [s] = [L].
Step 2: [ut] = [L T−1] × [T] = [L].
Step 3: [at²] = [L T−2] × [T²] = [L].
Result: all terms have dimension [L], so the equation passes the homogeneity test.

Counterexample

s = ut + at

The term at has dimension [L T⁻¹], not [L]; it cannot be added to the displacement term ut. This equation is dimensionally inconsistent.

Important limitation: dimensional consistency is necessary, but not sufficient, for a physically correct equation. Dimensions alone cannot establish pure numerical factors such as ½ or 2.

4. Derive simple formulas from dimensions

P-11-A-04 · Summative

Dimensional analysis can reveal how a physical quantity depends on selected variables. Begin with a product of powers, substitute dimensions, and compare the exponents of each base dimension.

Worked example — time period of a simple pendulum

Assume the time period T depends on the pendulum length ℓ, gravitational acceleration g, and bob mass m. We seek the dependence for small oscillations.

T = k ma ℓb gc

Here k is a dimensionless constant that cannot be obtained from dimensions alone.

Step 1 — substitute dimensions:
[T] = [M]a[L]b[L T−2]c
= [M]a[L]b+c[T]−2c
Step 2 — compare powers: mass: a = 0; length: b + c = 0; time: −2c = 1.
Step 3 — solve: c = −½ and b = +½.
T = k √(ℓ/g)

The dependence on bob mass vanishes (a = 0). Experiments or a physical derivation are needed to determine k; for small-angle simple harmonic motion, k = 2π.

Dimensional analysis generally cannot determine numerical constants or specific trigonometrical, logarithmic or exponential function shapes. A dimensionally correct formula still needs physical validation.

5. Accuracy versus precision

P-11-A-05 · Summative

Accuracy

How close a measurement is to an accepted reference or true value.

Precision

How closely repeated measurements agree with one another; an instrument's resolution can limit it.

An instrument can be highly precise yet consistently inaccurate if it has a calibration bias. Repeating a biased measurement does not necessarily remove its systematic error.

Example — three sets of mass readings

Suppose the reference mass is 160.0 g.

Readings (g)Interpretation
170.1, 169.9, 170.0Tightly clustered but far from the reference: precise, not accurate.
158, 162, 161Spread out, around the reference: less precise; average relatively close.
159.9, 160.0, 160.1Tightly clustered and near the reference: both accurate and precise.

Random variation makes repeated readings fluctuate; systematic error shifts results consistently. Good measurement practice includes calibration, appropriate instrument choice and repeated readings.

6. Measurement uncertainty

P-11-A-06 & P-11-A-07 · Summative

Every measured value is limited by the measuring instrument, observation method and conditions. Uncertainty expresses a defensible range around a reported measurement rather than claiming an exact value.

Measurement = (best estimate ± absolute uncertainty) unit

For example, (3.06 ± 0.02) mm states a measured size of 3.06 mm with an absolute uncertainty of 0.02 mm.

Uncertainty typeExpressionMeaning
AbsoluteΔxSame unit as measured quantity
Fractional (relative)Δx / |x|Dimensionless ratio, for x ≠ 0
Percentage(Δx / |x|) × 100%Relative uncertainty in percent
Worked example: x = (3.06 ± 0.02) mm. Percentage uncertainty = (0.02 ÷ 3.06) × 100% ≈ 0.65%.

Why uncertainty is unavoidable

Even careful measurements have finite instrument resolution, fluctuating environmental conditions, observer limitations and other possible variations. A reported uncertainty is part of a scientifically honest measurement, not evidence that the experiment has failed.

For the introductory rules below, use the simple uncertainty-propagation method taught in the supplied chapter. More advanced statistical approaches exist, but they are outside this guide's scope.

7. Uncertainty in calculated quantities

P-11-A-06 · Summative

Rule A — addition and subtraction

For a sum or difference of measured quantities, add their absolute uncertainties in the simple conservative method.

z = x ± y   ⇒   Δz = Δx + Δy
Example: x = (12.0 ± 0.2) cm, y = (5.0 ± 0.1) cm.
For x − y: z = 7.0 cm; Δz = 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.3 cm.
Result: (7.0 ± 0.3) cm.

Rule B — multiplication and division

For products or quotients, add fractional (or percentage) uncertainties.

z = xy or z = x/y   ⇒   Δz / |z| ≈ Δx / |x| + Δy / |y|
Example — resistance: V = (12.0 ± 0.2) V, I = (3.0 ± 0.1) A.
R = V/I = 12.0/3.0 = 4.0 Ω.
Relative uncertainty = 0.2/12.0 + 0.1/3.0 = 0.050 = 5.0%.
Absolute uncertainty in R = 0.05 × 4.0 = 0.20 Ω.
Result: (4.0 ± 0.2) Ω.

Rule C — a quantity raised to a power

If z = xn, the percentage uncertainty in z is approximately |n| times the percentage uncertainty in x (for small relative uncertainties).

z = xn   ⇒   Δz / |z| ≈ |n| × Δx / |x|
Example — area of a circle: r = (2.25 ± 0.01) cm and A = πr².
A ≈ 3.14159 × (2.25)² = 15.90 cm².
Percentage uncertainty in r ≈ (0.01/2.25) × 100% ≈ 0.444%.
Percentage uncertainty in A ≈ 2 × 0.444% = 0.889%.
ΔA ≈ 0.00889 × 15.90 ≈ 0.14 cm².
Result: (15.90 ± 0.14) cm².

Reporting a final answer

Keep sufficient digits during calculations, then round the result and uncertainty consistently. Units must accompany physical quantities. A precision claim should be justified by the original observations and instrument, not by extra calculator digits.

Self-check: ten original revision questions

Try these without looking back at the worked examples. The answers are below for self-assessment; these are study questions, not additional items added to our 275-MCQ bank.

  1. Explain why a carefully reasoned estimate is different from a random guess. Is P-11-A-01 formative or summative?
  2. Write the SI base units and dimensions of pressure.
  3. Check whether s = ut + at is dimensionally homogeneous when s is displacement, u velocity, a acceleration and t time.
  4. Why can dimensional analysis not determine the factor ½ in a kinematics equation?
  5. A balance repeatedly reads 52.1 g, 52.0 g and 52.1 g for a 50.0 g reference mass. Is it precise, accurate, both or neither?
  6. Calculate the percentage uncertainty for (20.0 ± 0.1) cm.
  7. Find the result and absolute uncertainty when (10.0 ± 0.2) cm is reduced by (4.0 ± 0.1) cm.
  8. If z = x², approximately how does its percentage uncertainty compare with that of x?
  9. A simple pendulum's length is doubled, with g unchanged. By what factor does its period change according to T ∝ √(ℓ/g)?
  10. Give two practical reasons why even a carefully measured quantity has uncertainty.
