The textbook calculation

Source approaches stationary listener
f′ = f × v / (v − vs)

Closer wavefronts → higher observed frequency.

Source recedes from stationary listener
f′ = f × v / (v + vs)

More distant wavefronts → lower observed frequency.

Here f is emitted frequency, v is speed of sound in stationary air, and vs is the source speed (taken as a positive magnitude). This laboratory treats a moving source and a stationary observer only, matching the required FBISE numerical scope.

Why are approach and recede shifts unequal?

The denominators are v − vs and v + vs. Their ratios are not symmetrical about the emitted frequency.

At vs = 0, the three frequencies become identical. This is a useful control test.

Why Doppler shifts matter

These examples come from the NBF chapter's applications; the numerical formula used in this laboratory applies directly to a moving sound source and stationary listener, not automatically to reflected radar signals or light.

🚑 Ambulance or bus siren

Higher pitch on approach and lower pitch as the source recedes. Students can hear the idealized difference.

🚢 SONAR

Changes in reflected sound frequency can help identify the motion of a vessel or underwater target.

📡 Radar and satellites

Radio-wave frequency shifts can be used to study speed and direction; their equations differ from this moving-sound-source model.

🌌 Astronomy

Redshift and blueshift reveal relative motion of astronomical sources; these involve electromagnetic waves.

🩺 Medical Doppler ultrasound

Echo frequency changes help estimate blood-flow speed and direction.

🌧 Weather radar

Frequency shifts in returned electromagnetic signals help meteorologists measure motion.

Important: changing pitch does not mean the ambulance changes the siren's emitted frequency. The difference arises because a stationary listener receives wavefronts at a different rate. The speed of sound in the air remains approximately the same in this ideal model.
