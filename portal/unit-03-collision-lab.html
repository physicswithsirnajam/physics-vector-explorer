Air Resistance & Collision Explorer

Compare an ideal projectile with one affected by air resistance, then investigate how masses and velocities change the outcome of a head-on collision. The lab emphasizes the distinction between conserved momentum and possibly changing kinetic energy.

Learning boundaries: Air resistance is shown with a qualitative schematic, not a calculated drag trajectory. Collisions are modelled as one-dimensional and isolated. The perfectly inelastic option means the bodies stick together, which is one special case of an inelastic collision. This public activity neither grades students nor saves results to their accounts.

1. How air resistance changes projectile motion

In the absence of air resistance, only gravity acts after launch. Air resistance acts opposite to the projectile’s instantaneous velocity and generally reduces range and maximum height for otherwise identical conditions.

3. What this does and does not model

Source coverage: NBF Physics XI Unit 3 pp. 42–49 (air resistance, conservation of momentum and collisions), and FBISE Grade XI Physics Assessment Framework SLOs P-11-B-06 and P-11-B-11 through P-11-B-13.

This laboratory does not calculate numerical air drag, off-centre collisions or two-dimensional momentum. Only ideal instantaneous head-on collision cases are calculated. The source textbook’s general description of inelastic collisions does not imply all inelastic bodies must stick; the “stick” option is explicitly a special case added for teaching.
