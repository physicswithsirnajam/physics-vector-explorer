7.3.1 · NBF printed p. 122 · P-11-B-25

Measuring Young's modulus of a metal wire

Measure a test wire's original length L and diameter at several positions, then calculate its cross-sectional area A = πd²/4. Hang small increasing loads on the wire, measure each additional extension x against a parallel reference wire and vernier scale, and record the corresponding applied tensile force F. Keep loads within the wire's linear elastic range.

Y = FL / (Ax)F: applied force (N) · L: original length (m) · A: wire area (m²) · x: measured extension (m) · Y: Pa

Observe and calculate

Plot force F against extension x in the proportional range. Its slope F/x = k gives wire stiffness. Hence Y = (L/A)k.

Control experimental error

Take several readings, average the measured diameter carefully, avoid parallax, allow the wire to settle and check that unloading restores the original length. Repeat loads to identify the linear region.

Important distinction: Use the measured applied force and its corresponding extension when determining Young's modulus. The average force F/2 is used to calculate the work of stretching from zero to F when the force rises linearly—not to replace F in the Young's modulus formula.

7.3.2 · NBF printed pp. 122–123 · P-11-B-27

Work from the force–extension graph

For a gradually stretched wire, the force changes with extension. The small work done during an additional extension dx is dW = F(x) dx. Therefore, total work equals the area under the F-versus-x curve, with extension on the horizontal axis and force on the vertical axis.

W = ∫ F(x) dxArea under force (N) versus extension (m) · Unit: N m = J

If the wire obeys Hooke's law, F = kx, while being stretched from zero to a final extension x, the graph is a straight line through the origin. Its triangular area gives work W = ½Fx. This triangular rule should not be applied automatically to a nonlinear force–extension curve.

Textbook diagram clarification: NBF Figure 7.14 places weight on the horizontal axis and extension vertically. That orientation is not the conventional graph for finding work as the area under force versus extension. The original graph below uses force vertically and extension horizontally. In the special straight-line case, the textbook's triangle happens to give the same number, but the integral for work is ∫F dx.

7.3.3 · NBF printed pp. 123–124 · P-11-B-28

Derivation of elastic potential energy

While the material stays in its limit of proportionality and returns elastically, work done against its restoring force is stored as elastic potential energy U. Starting from Hooke's law, we obtain:

1 · Area under the graph

U = ½FxF is the final force, x is the final extension

For linear loading from rest, the average force is F/2, so work = (F/2)x.

2 · Introduce Hooke's law

U = ½kx²Since F = kx; k is in N/m

Doubling extension within the same linear range makes the stored energy four times as large.

3 · Express in terms of Young's modulus

U = YA x² / (2L)Since k = YA/L; Y in Pa, A in m², L and x in m

This is the strain energy in a uniform wire, assuming small linear-elastic extension.

Stress–strain area nuance: The area under a stress (Pa) versus strain (dimensionless) graph represents strain energy per unit volume (J/m³), not total energy in joules. For a uniformly strained wire, multiply by volume AL to obtain total stored energy.

Source-audited NBF Example 7.3

The book's steel-wire example uses F = 980 N, L = 1 m, diameter d = 0.004 m, and Y = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa. The correct radius is r = d/2 = 0.002 m, so area A ≈ 1.257 × 10⁻⁵ m², extension x ≈ 3.90 × 10⁻⁴ m, and stored energy U ≈ 0.191 J.

NBF printed p. 123 incorrectly labels the radius as 0.004 m in its Given section; the p. 124 area calculation correctly uses 0.002 m. This is a documented textbook notation inconsistency, not a change to the original problem data.

Scientific source literacy

Two important textbook clarifications

Figure 7.14 axis choice · p. 122

NBF sketches extension against weight. For calculating work by integrating the loading force, place extension horizontally and force vertically. For a proportional (straight-line) load the triangular product happens to match either orientation numerically, but that is not true for arbitrary nonlinear curves.

Example 7.3 diameter/radius · pp. 123–124

A 0.004 m diameter means 0.002 m radius. Use A = π(0.002 m)². The original Given line calls 0.004 m the radius, while the subsequent area computation uses the correct radius.

A further distinction from NBF p. 124 summary: total strain energy for a uniform sample equals volume multiplied by the area under a stress–strain curve; the graph's area alone is energy density.

Self-check · Concept review, not an official MCQ bank

Test your understanding

1. Which quantities belong on the axes when the area under the graph gives work?

Plot force F on the vertical axis against extension x on the horizontal axis. Work is the integral ∫F dx.

2. Why is the area triangular for a Hooke's-law wire?

Force increases linearly from 0 to F while extension increases from 0 to x, so the area is ½Fx.

3. What happens to elastic potential energy if extension is doubled, with the same k?

U = ½kx², so energy is four times as large, provided the wire remains within the proportional range.

4. What is the correct radius if a wire's diameter is 4.0 mm?

2.0 mm = 0.002 m; calculate area using this radius, not the diameter.

5. Is average force F/2 used to find Young's modulus?

No. Young's modulus uses the actual load F and its measured extension x: Y = FL/(Ax). The average F/2 is used for the work ½Fx when loading is linear from zero.

6. What does the area under a stress–strain curve represent?

Energy per unit volume (J/m³) in the elastic portion; multiply by specimen volume to find total energy, if the strain is uniform.
