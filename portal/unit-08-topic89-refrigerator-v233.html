8.9 · A reversed heat engine

A refrigerator is a thermodynamic device that extracts thermal energy from a lower-temperature region and rejects energy to higher-temperature surroundings by using external work. Its working fluid (refrigerant) circulates through a cycle.

In a heat engine, some heat from a hot source becomes work output. In a refrigerator or heat pump, the direction of useful energy transfer is reversed: work is supplied to move heat from the colder space to a warmer one.

Cold compartment TC

Heat QC is removed from the cooled region.

Working refrigerant + work Win

External mechanical or electrical work powers heat transport.

Warm surroundings TH

Total heat QH is released to the room.

Clausius statement: Cold-to-hot heat transfer is possible here because external work is supplied. The refrigerator does not move heat uphill in temperature as the sole effect of a process.

Source: original NBF printed p.145, Section 8.9 and Figure 8.18. The diagram above uses original text boxes rather than copying the publisher figure.

Energy balance of a refrigerator

QC and QH are positive heat magnitudes: QC is heat removed from the cold compartment and QH is heat delivered to the warmer room. Win is the positive work supplied to the device.

QH = QC + WinEquivalent: Win = QH − QC for a complete cycle.

The heat reaching the room is greater than the electrical or mechanical work supplied, since it includes the heat extracted from the cooled region. This is why the heating effect can exceed the work input without creating energy.

Do not confuse signs: The external work Win enters the device. In Topic 8.4's First Law with work by the system, the same work transfer would have Wby = −Win.

Coefficient of Performance (COP)

A COP measures useful heat transferred per unit work supplied. Cooling and heating use the same physical device but select different useful effects, so their COPs are not equal.

Cooling · Refrigerator

COPcool = QC/WinUseful cooling: heat removed from the colder space.
COPcool,ideal = TC/(TH − TC)Reversible Carnot limit; absolute temperature in kelvin.

Heating · Heat pump

COPheat = QH/WinUseful heating: heat supplied to the warmer room.
COPheat,ideal = TH/(TH − TC)Reversible Carnot limit; absolute temperature in kelvin.

The relationship

COPheat = COPcool + 1

Follows from QH = QC + Win divided by Win. It applies to the same cyclic energy flows, not only to a Carnot device.

Important: A COP greater than 1 is possible because the device moves heat; it is not a claim of more than 100% energy conversion. The temperature-ratio formulas represent ideal reversible limits and must not be used as measured performance figures for real fridges. Always convert Celsius to kelvin before substitution.

Textbook Example 8.5 · Independently solved adaptation

Source data: A refrigerator maintains its cold region at 6 °C while the room is at 37 °C. Under the textbook's ideal assumption, determine the heat delivered to the room for each joule of electricity consumed.
Reveal the ideal temperature, work and heat steps
  1. Using the textbook's rounded Celsius-to-kelvin conversion, TC = 6 + 273 = 279 K and TH = 37 + 273 = 310 K.
  2. Ideal cooling COP = TC/(TH − TC) = 279/(310 − 279) = 279/31 = 9.
  3. For Win = 1 J, the heat removed is QC = 9 × 1 J = 9 J.
  4. The heat delivered to the room is QH = QC + Win = 9 J + 1 J = 10 J.

Answer: ideally, 10 J of heat reaches the room for every 1 J of electricity used. The extra 9 J comes from the cooled region; no energy is created.

Original Example 8.5 is on printed pp.146–147. All explanation and algebraic steps here are independent educational adaptations.

Textbook Assignment 8.5 · Symbolic solution

Source data: The refrigerator interior is at t2 °C, and the room is at t1 °C. Find the ideal heat delivered to the room per joule of electricity supplied. The textbook gives symbols here, not fixed numeric values.
Reveal the symbolic derivation
  1. Assume the room is warmer than the cooled compartment: t1 > t2, with absolute temperatures above zero.
  2. Write TH = t1 + 273.15 and TC = t2 + 273.15, both in kelvin.
  3. The ideal heating COP is QH/Win = TH/(TH − TC).
  4. For Win = 1 J, substitute: QH = (t1 + 273.15)/(t1 − t2) × 1 J.

Answer: QH = [(t1 + 273.15)/(t1 − t2)] J of ideal heat delivered per 1 J of electricity. A numerical result would require the actual values of t1 and t2.

Five conceptual checks · Reveal independent explanations

1. Why does a refrigerator not contradict the Clausius statement?
A refrigerator transports heat from cold to hot with an external work input; it does not do so with no other effect.
2. What is the difference between cooling COP and heating COP?
Cooling COP measures the heat removed from the cold region per unit work. Heating COP measures heat delivered to the hot region per unit work. For the same cycle, COP heating = COP cooling + 1.
3. A refrigerator removes 9 J of heat for 1 J of work. How much heat is rejected to the room?
QH = QC + Win = 9 + 1 = 10 J.
4. Does a cooling COP of 9 mean the device creates nine joules of energy?
No. It removes nine joules of heat from the cooled space for each joule of work and rejects ten joules to the room. Total energy is conserved.
5. What is required to turn Assignment 8.5's symbolic expression into a number?
Numerical values of the room temperature t1 and the refrigerator temperature t2 must be supplied. The original textbook does not give those values in Assignment 8.5.
