8.2 · Kinetic Theory of Gases

Model assumptions

  • A sample contains a huge number of particles.
  • The particles are much smaller than their typical separation, so their own occupied volume can be neglected in this idealization.
  • Particles move randomly and continually; collisions with walls and other molecules are treated as perfectly elastic.
  • Except during collisions, the ideal model neglects intermolecular attractive and repulsive forces.

Source orientation: original NBF printed p.131; the textbook's Figure 8.2 is not reproduced.

What creates gas pressure?

Gas molecules continually collide with the container walls, changing their momentum. The combined force from those collisions produces pressure. Higher temperature at fixed volume generally increases pressure because molecular motion becomes more energetic.

Limits of the model: At sufficiently high gas density or low temperature, molecular volume and intermolecular attraction become important, and an ideal-gas prediction may be inaccurate.

NBF pp.131–132. This is a model description, not a claim that real-gas particles have zero size.

Linking pressure to density

We can combine the ideal-gas law, pV = nRT, with the molar mass of a sample. If the gas has mass m and molar mass M, then n = m/M and mass density ρ = m/V.

Number of moles

n = m/M

Use kilograms for sample mass and kilograms per mole for molar mass, or a consistent equivalent pair of mass units.

Ideal-gas density

ρ = pM/(RT)Equivalently: p = ρRT/M

ρ: kg m−3; p: Pa; M: kg mol−1; R: J mol−1 K−1; T: kelvin.

Interpretation: at constant gas pressure and composition, increasing absolute temperature lowers density. At fixed temperature, increasing pressure raises density within the ideal-gas approximation.

8.2.1–8.2.2 · Work done by and on a gas

Imagine a cylinder with a freely moving piston. The gas pushes against a fixed external pressure while the piston moves. During a constant-pressure expansion, the work done by the gas equals the pressure multiplied by the volume change.

Won = −pΔV = −WbyFor the same boundary movement, work on the gas has the opposite sign

Graph interpretation: for a constant-pressure process, the magnitude of the work is the area of a rectangle under the pressure–volume graph. For a quasi-static process with varying pressure, the signed work done by the gas is Wby = ∫p dV. Area depends on correct axes and the direction of the process. A mere point on the graph is not a work value.

NBF Worked Example 8.2 · Source-data adaptation

Original printed p.133. Original data and physical task retained; the solution and wording here are independently prepared rather than copied from the publisher.

Given: argon gas mass m = 2 kg, cylinder radius r = 1 m and height h = 2 m, gas temperature 30 °C ≈ 303 K, argon molar mass M ≈ 0.040 kg mol−1. Find: the absolute gas pressure using ideal-gas assumptions.
  1. Convert to moles: n = m/M = (2 kg)/(0.040 kg mol−1) = 50 mol.
  2. Calculate cylinder volume: V = πr2h = π(1 m)2(2 m) ≈ 6.283 m³.
  3. Use the ideal-gas equation: p = nRT/V, with R ≈ 8.314 J mol−1 K−1.
  4. Substitute: p = [(50 mol)(8.314 J mol−1 K−1)(303 K)]/(6.283 m³).
p ≈ 2.00 × 104 PaApproximately 20.0 kPa; independently calculated value is 20,047 Pa with the stated rounded constants.

This is an ideal-gas estimate. The 2 kg sample is treated as pure argon, with no real-gas interaction correction.

NBF Assignment 8.2 · Compression and the work sign

Original printed p.133. Source data preserved: a gas compressed to one-third its initial volume, p = 2.25 × 105 Pa, with 200 J work done on the gas. The walkthrough is a teacher-review adaptation.

Task: Find the final volume when Vf = Vi/3 and Won = +200 J at constant pressure.
Reveal the independent step-by-step calculation
  1. Let initial volume be Vi. Final volume Vf = Vi/3.
  2. Hence ΔV = Vf − Vi = −(2/3)Vi, a negative change because the gas is compressed.
  3. Work done on the gas is positive in compression: Won = −pΔV = p(2/3)Vi.
  4. Rearrange: Vi = (3Won)/(2p) = (3 × 200 J)/(2 × 2.25 × 105 Pa) ≈ 1.333 × 10−3 m³.
  5. Vf = Vi/3 ≈ 4.444 × 10−4 m³.
  6. Check: ΔV ≈ −8.889 × 10−4 m³; Wby = pΔV ≈ −200 J; Won ≈ +200 J.

Vf ≈ 4.44 × 10−4 m³ = 0.444 L

The distinct signs for work by versus work on the gas are essential. Both are not positive for the same compression.

Four conceptual self-checks · No grade

1. Why do gas molecules exert pressure on a container wall?
Their collisions change momentum and exert forces on the walls. The total force per unit area is pressure.
2. Why does the ideal-gas model become less reliable at high density or very low temperature?
Finite molecular size and intermolecular forces become important; the assumptions of negligible size and negligible forces may fail.
3. A gas at 100 kPa expands by 2 L against constant pressure. What are the signs and values of work?
Wby = +(100 kPa)(2 L) = +200 J; Won = −200 J.
4. The gas is compressed at constant pressure. Can work by the gas and work on the gas both be positive?
No. For the same boundary movement, Wby = pΔV and Won = −pΔV. During compression ΔV < 0, so work by the gas is negative and work on it is positive.
