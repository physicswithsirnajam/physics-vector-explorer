2. Uniform acceleration in a straight line

Choose a positive direction. Let u be initial velocity, v final velocity, a constant signed acceleration, t time and s signed displacement.

v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as

The velocity–time graph is a straight line when acceleration is constant. Its gradient gives a; the signed area below the graph gives s. The graph explains the equations as a formative derivation (P-11-B-03). In summative numerical problems (P-11-B-04), identify the known and unknown quantities, choose a suitable equation, substitute and interpret the sign.

Formative derivation from the graph

Show the reasoning (P-11-B-03 · formative)

From the slope of a velocity–time line: a = (v−u)/t. Rearranging gives v = u + at.

The trapezoid area for signed displacement is s = ½(u+v)t. Substituting v = u + at gives s = ut + ½at².

Eliminating t between v = u + at and s = ½(u+v)t gives v² = u² + 2as.

Exam distinction: learn the reasoning to understand motion, but the framework lists this derivation as formative, not summative theory.

Numerical practice from the textbook

Textbook Example 3.1 (p. 35)

A bus has u = 10 m/s, constant a = 2 m/s² and t = 5 s. Find its final velocity.

Reveal step-by-step solution

v = u + at = 10 + (2)(5) = 20 m/s.

Check: velocity increases because acceleration is positive.

Textbook Example 3.2 (p. 36)

A car has u = 4 m/s, a = 1 m/s² and t = 10 s. Calculate the distance travelled in the stated constant-acceleration motion.

Reveal step-by-step solution

s = ut + ½at² = (4)(10) + ½(1)(10²) = 40 + 50 = 90 m.

Because velocity remains positive throughout, distance equals displacement here.

NBF printed pp. 33–36, sections 3.1.1–3.1.3, Examples 3.1 and 3.2.

3. Free fall and sign conventions

For ideal free fall without air resistance, acceleration has constant magnitude g = 9.8 m/s² near Earth’s surface. The sign depends on your chosen axis: upward-positive implies a = −g, while downward-positive implies a = +g.

With upward-positive: vᵧ = uᵧ − gt;   y = uᵧt − ½gt²

For an object simply released from rest, uᵧ = 0. For an object thrown upward, velocity decreases to zero at the highest point, then becomes negative on descent.

Textbook section 3.1.4, printed pp. 36–37. The ideal model neglects drag.

4. Projectile motion without air resistance

For a projectile launched at speed u at angle θ above the horizontal, its components evolve independently: horizontal acceleration is zero while vertical acceleration is −g.

uₓ = u cos θ;   uᵧ = u sin θ
x = (u cos θ)t;   y = (u sin θ)t − ½gt²
vₓ = u cos θ;   vᵧ = u sin θ − gt

For launch and landing at the same level:

Time of flight T = 2u sin θ/g
Maximum height H = u²sin²θ/(2g)
Horizontal range R = u²sin(2θ)/g

At fixed u and g, the greatest same-level range occurs at 45°. Complementary angles θ and (90°−θ) produce the same range without air resistance. A horizontal launch from a cliff needs a different flight-time equation and is not covered by the equal-height shortcut above.

Textbook Example 3.5 (p. 43)

A projectile is launched with initial speed 200 m/s at 30° above horizontal; neglect air resistance and assume equal start/end levels. Determine T, H and R.

Reveal textbook-source calculation

Vertical initial velocity: uᵧ = 200 sin 30° = 100 m/s.

Flight time: T = 2(100)/9.8 = 20.41 s.

Maximum height: H = 100²/(2×9.8) = 510.2 m.

Range: R = (200² sin 60°)/9.8 ≈ 3534.8 m (rounding may differ slightly by trig approximation).

Original complement-angle practice

Keeping u and g fixed, compare launching at 30° and 60°. Does the range change?

Reveal reasoning

R = u²sin(2θ)/g. At 30°: sin 60°. At 60°: sin 120° = sin 60°.

The ranges are equal, but flight time and maximum height are not generally equal.

Textbook sections 3.2.1–3.2.2, printed pp. 37–43. Exercise treatment of air resistance belongs to summative SLO P-11-B-06, not to this no-drag simulator.
