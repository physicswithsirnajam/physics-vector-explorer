8.8 · The ideal Carnot engine

A Carnot engine is a theoretical heat engine operating in an entirely reversible cycle between a hot reservoir at TH and a cold reservoir at TC. Its working substance returns to its starting thermodynamic state after a complete cycle.

The textbook describes the Carnot cycle using two isothermal and two adiabatic reversible processes. The net work delivered by the cycle equals the area enclosed by its pressure–volume loop in the ideal model.

Keep the concepts separate: A four-stroke petrol engine (Topic 8.5) is not a Carnot cycle. Carnot's model is an ideal maximum-efficiency benchmark for engines between two specified temperatures.

Original NBF printed p.143, Section 8.8 and Figures 8.16–8.17, interpreted in original words rather than reproduced graphics.

8.8.1 · Efficiency of a Carnot engine

A heat engine receives a positive amount of heat QH from the hot source and rejects a positive amount QC to a cooler sink. For its complete cycle, ΔU = 0, so net work is the difference:

Wnet,out = QH − QCHeat amounts QH, QC are positive magnitudes in joules.
η = Wnet,out / QH = 1 − QC / QHEnergy-efficiency relationship for a cyclic heat engine with these heat transfers.

For the ideal reversible Carnot cycle, the heat-transfer ratio is fixed by its two reservoir temperatures, measured on the absolute kelvin scale:

ηCarnot = 1 − TC / THTH and TC are positive absolute temperatures (K); TH > TC.

Multiply by 100 to express the efficiency as a percentage. Use kelvin, not raw Celsius numbers, in the temperature ratio. When TH and TC get closer, the maximum possible efficiency decreases.

Ideal limit: The formula would give 100% if TC = 0 K, but absolute zero is not a physically attainable cold reservoir. A real engine also has irreversible losses, so it falls below the Carnot benchmark for the same reservoir temperatures.

Original NBF pp.144–145, textbook equations 8.8–8.9 and percentage-efficiency form.

8.8.2 · Carnot's theorem

No heat engine operating between the same two reservoir temperatures can be more efficient than a reversible Carnot engine. Every reversible engine operating between the same two ideal reservoirs has the same theoretical efficiency, independent of its particular working substance.

To compare two engines fairly, first check that their hot and cold reservoir temperatures are the same. The Carnot efficiency is an upper bound, not a promise of achievable practical performance.

Original NBF printed p.145, Carnot's theorem and following discussion.

Textbook Example 8.4 · Independently solved adaptation

Source data: Hot reservoir at 400 °C; cold reservoir at 30 °C. Calculate the maximum theoretical Carnot efficiency.
Reveal conversion and mathematical steps
  1. Convert both temperatures to absolute units using the textbook's rounded conversion: TH = 400 + 273 = 673 K; TC = 30 + 273 = 303 K.
  2. For a reversible Carnot cycle, η = 1 − TC/TH = 1 − 303/673.
  3. Compute η ≈ 0.5498, so η × 100 ≈ 55% to the textbook's reported precision.

Answer: approximately 55%. This is a maximum ideal efficiency between the specified reservoir temperatures, not a measured real-engine result.

Original Example 8.4 appears on NBF printed p.145; independent prose and calculations, textbook rounded kelvin convention retained.

Textbook Assignment 8.4 · Independently solved adaptation

Source data: A heat engine has thermal efficiency 40% and receives 4 kJ of heat from its heat source. Find the magnitude of the waste heat rejected.
Reveal heat balance and solution
  1. η = 40/100 = 0.40; heat input QH = 4 kJ.
  2. Wnet,out = ηQH = 0.40 × 4 kJ = 1.6 kJ.
  3. For the cyclic engine, QC = QH − Wnet,out = 4.0 − 1.6 = 2.4 kJ.

Answer: the engine rejects 2.4 kJ of waste heat. This heat amount is a positive magnitude directed toward the sink.

Original Assignment 8.4 on printed p.145. Unlike Example 8.4, this problem states general heat-engine efficiency; no reservoir temperatures or Carnot assumption are required.

Five concept checks · Answers on demand

1. Which two processes exchange heat with reservoirs in the Carnot cycle?
The two isothermal processes: expansion at the high temperature absorbs heat, and compression at the low temperature rejects heat. The two adiabatic steps have no heat transfer.
2. Does an engine with 80% efficiency always violate Carnot's theorem?
Not necessarily. Compare it with the Carnot limit for its actual reservoir temperatures. An 80% engine is impossible between reservoirs whose Carnot efficiency is below 80%.
3. Can 400 °C and 30 °C be substituted directly into the Carnot temperature ratio?
No. Convert to kelvin first. The textbook gives TH ≈ 673 K and TC ≈ 303 K; η ≈ 55%.
4. A cycle receives 4 kJ and converts 40% to net work. What happens to the rest?
Work = 1.6 kJ. The remaining 2.4 kJ is rejected to the lower-temperature sink.
5. Why is the Carnot engine considered an ideal benchmark?
It follows entirely reversible processes. No engine operating between the same two reservoirs can exceed its theoretical efficiency; real dissipative processes reduce achievable performance.
