8.10 · What does entropy describe?

Entropy (S) is a thermodynamic state property associated with the dispersal of thermal energy and how energy is distributed among the possible microscopic arrangements of a system. The NBF textbook introduces it qualitatively through particle disorder/randomness and the reduction in energy available to do useful work.

As ice melts, molecules become less constrained than in the solid; ordinary mixing and dispersal often increase entropy. These everyday examples help develop intuition, but entropy is not simply a visual count of messiness: its precise thermodynamic meaning comes from heat transfer along a reversible path and its state-function properties.

Important distinction: The Second Law says entropy of an isolated system cannot decrease. In an irreversible process, the combined entropy of the system and its surroundings increases. A system exchanging heat with its environment can have negative entropy change without violating the Second Law.

8.10.1 · Sign convention for entropy change

For a reversible heat transfer that takes place at a constant absolute boundary temperature, the entropy change of the system is:

ΔS = Qrev / TQrev is signed reversible heat in joules (J); T is absolute temperature in kelvin (K); ΔS is measured in joules per kelvin (J/K).
Heat crossing system boundaryQrev signΔS for the system
Heat is absorbedPositive (+)Positive: entropy increases
Heat is released or removedNegative (−)Negative: entropy decreases
No reversible heat transferZeroZero for a reversible adiabatic process
Scope: ΔS = Qrev/T is used here for an isothermal reversible transfer. For changing temperature, the corresponding thermodynamic expression is an integral of δQrev/T along a reversible path. Do not apply the simple quotient to an arbitrary irreversible process.

8.10.2 · Entropy, energy quality and the arrow of time

The textbook connects entropy to the degradation of energy: total energy is conserved by the First Law, but in real processes energy often becomes more widely dispersed and less available for useful work. This is why a heat engine must reject some energy to its surroundings.

Heat passes spontaneously from hotter to colder objects, not the reverse as the sole effect. Everyday dissipative processes such as friction and mixing help explain the arrow of time: naturally occurring changes have a preferred direction.

System versus total: For a reversible process, the combined entropy change of system plus surroundings is zero. For an irreversible process in an isolated whole, it is positive. Neither statement requires the entropy of the individual subsystem to increase every time.

Original Example 8.6 · Independently worked adaptation

Source data: 700 g of ice melts at 0 °C. Latent heat of fusion of ice is 3.36 × 105 J/kg. Calculate entropy change during the isothermal phase transition.
Reveal conversion, heat and entropy steps
  1. Convert mass: m = 700 g = 0.700 kg.
  2. Use the source's rounded absolute temperature: T = 0 + 273 = 273 K.
  3. Heat absorbed to melt ice: Qrev = mLf = 0.700 × 3.36 × 105 = 235,200 J.
  4. Entropy change: ΔS = Qrev/T = 235,200/273 ≈ +861.5 J/K.

Answer: ΔS ≈ +861.5 J/K. Positive because the ice absorbs heat and its entropy increases during melting.

Original Assignment 8.6 · Independently worked adaptation

Source data: A body is at 150 °C during a reversible isothermal process, and 7,373 J of heat is removed. Find the entropy change of the body.
Reveal heat-flow sign and steps
  1. The body loses heat, so heat transferred into the body is Qrev = −7,373 J.
  2. Using rounded textbook kelvin conversion, T = 150 + 273 = 423 K.
  3. ΔS = Qrev/T = −7,373/423 ≈ −17.43 J/K.

Answer: ΔS ≈ −17.4 J/K. The body's entropy decreases because heat leaves it. This alone does not imply that the total entropy of the surroundings and body decreases.

Four conceptual situations · Reveal the interpretation

The system's entropy increases

Reversible melting at a fixed temperature absorbs latent heat. The ice/water system gains entropy as the solid turns to liquid.

Five self-checks · Open the explanation

1. What are the SI units of entropy and entropy change?
J/K (joules per kelvin). For reversible isothermal heat transfer, ΔS=Qrev/T.
2. Why is entropy change positive during melting in Example 8.6?
The melting ice absorbs positive latent heat at fixed temperature, so its ΔS is positive: approximately +861.5 J/K with the textbook's numbers.
3. If heat is removed from a body reversibly at constant temperature, what is the sign of ΔS?
Negative for the body because Qrev is negative. Assignment 8.6 gives about −17.4 J/K.
4. Can the entropy of one system decrease while the Second Law still holds?
Yes. A body may lose heat and entropy to its environment. The nondecreasing entropy requirement applies to the combined isolated whole, not to every subsystem.
5. Does a reversible process always leave the system's own entropy unchanged?
No. Reversible heat transfer can change the system's entropy. For an ideal reversible process, it is the combined entropy of system plus surroundings that has zero net change.
