8.1 · Thermal equilibrium

When do two bodies reach equilibrium?

Place two bodies at different temperatures in thermal contact through a heat-conducting material. Energy moves spontaneously from the hotter body to the cooler one until they reach the same temperature. At that point there is no net heat transfer between the bodies.

Key distinction: equal temperatures mean no net heat flow; they do not imply that molecules stop moving.

System, surroundings and boundary

  • System: the chosen matter or region being studied.
  • Surroundings: everything outside that system.
  • Open: matter and energy may cross the boundary.
  • Closed: energy may cross, but matter does not.
  • Isolated (idealization): neither matter nor energy crosses.

Textbook orientation: printed p.128, Figure 8.1(a) and system-types note. No publisher artwork has been copied.

Scientific wording note. The book introduces heat in simplified language. In precise thermodynamics, heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference, while internal energy is a property of the system. Heat is not a quantity stored inside an object.

8.1.1 · Internal energy

Molecular meaning

Internal energy includes microscopic kinetic and interaction potential energies. The ideal-gas model neglects intermolecular interaction potential energy (apart from collisions), so its internal energy depends on temperature. For a real gas, internal energy can also depend on volume.

⟨Etr⟩ = ½(3) kBT = 3⁄2 kBT Average translational energy per ideal-gas molecule · T in kelvin

State function and energy change

The system's internal-energy change depends on its initial and final states, not the intermediate path:

ΔU = Ufinal − Uinitial

For an ideal gas, temperature rise implies ΔU > 0 and unchanged temperature implies ΔU = 0. Heat transfer and work can both change internal energy. Work and heat individually depend on the process path.

8.1.2 · Ideal gas equation and applications

The textbook presents two equivalent ideal-gas forms. Use the number of moles for the first or the number of molecules for the second.

Macroscopic form

pV = nRT

p: absolute pressure (Pa) · V: volume (m³) · n: amount in moles (mol) · R ≈ 8.314 J mol−1 K−1 · T: kelvin (K).

Molecular form

pV = NkBT

N: number of molecules (dimensionless). Since n = N/NA, the Boltzmann constant is kB = R/NA ≈ 1.380649 × 10−23 J K−1.

Remember: pressure must be absolute; all temperatures must be in kelvin. We use the ideal-gas approximation, which is most reliable when intermolecular effects and molecular sizes are negligible in the gas state under consideration.

Original textbook Example 8.1 · Teacher-worked adaptation

Source: NBF printed pp.130–131. Problem theme: average translational kinetic energy at room temperature. Original text is paraphrased; solution independently recalculated.

Given: room temperature of 25 °C ≈ 298 K and kB ≈ 1.38 × 10−23 J K−1. Find: mean translational kinetic energy of one molecule.
  1. Convert temperature to kelvin: T ≈ 25 + 273 = 298 K.
  2. Use ⟨Etr⟩ = (3/2)kBT.
  3. Substitute: (3/2)(1.38 × 10−23 J K−1)(298 K).
  4. The K units cancel. The average translational kinetic energy is:
⟨Etr⟩ ≈ 6.17 × 10−21 J

This is the average translational kinetic energy per molecule, not the total internal energy of a mole of a polyatomic gas.

Task: Estimate the total number of air molecules in a 20.0 m³ room at 27 °C and 1 atm, treating the room air as an ideal-gas mixture.
Show the step-by-step teacher-review solution
  1. Convert pressure: p ≈ 1.01325 × 105 Pa.
  2. Convert temperature: T ≈ 27 + 273 = 300 K.
  3. Choose the molecular form: N = pV/(kBT).
  4. Substitute: N = [(1.01325 × 105 Pa)(20.0 m³)] / [(1.380649 × 10−23 J K−1)(300 K)].
  5. Evaluate: N ≈ 4.89 × 1026 molecules.

Quick self-check · No grade recorded

1. Two bodies are at the same temperature and in thermal contact. Must net heat flow between them?
No. At thermal equilibrium there is no net heat flow between the bodies, even though microscopic motion continues.
2. Why is internal energy called a state function?
ΔU depends on the initial and final states. Different paths between the same states can have different heat and work transfers but the same ΔU.
3. Which ideal-gas expression would you choose if you know the number of molecules instead of moles?
Use pV = NkBT, where kB = R/NA. Always use absolute pressure and kelvin temperature.
4. If absolute temperature rises from 300 K to 600 K for an ideal-gas sample, what happens to average translational kinetic energy per molecule?
It doubles, since ⟨Etr⟩ = (3/2)kBT is directly proportional to absolute temperature.
