Weightlessness in Satellites & Artificial Gravity

Why do astronauts appear to float even though Earth's gravity still acts? And how could a rotating space station make the floor feel like the ground? The key ideas are free fall, support force, and centripetal acceleration.

P-11-B-16 · SUMMATIVE

1. Why do astronauts appear weightless in an orbiting satellite?

Even a satellite hundreds of kilometres above Earth is affected by Earth's gravitational attraction. For a near-circular orbit, gravity provides the inward acceleration that continuously changes the satellite's direction. The astronaut and spacecraft are in free fall together around Earth.

When a freely floating astronaut is not pressing against the cabin floor, the floor supplies approximately zero normal support force. The astronaut therefore experiences approximately zero apparent weight, despite having mass and being acted upon by gravity.

Key distinction: Gravity is not zero in orbit. Weightlessness means a near-absence of supporting contact force. It is not the absence of gravitational attraction.

Why does the textbook also mention an inertial or centrifugal effect?

The NBF section describes the orbital free-fall effect partly using the rotating/orbital reference-frame language of an inertial effect balancing gravity. This is a frame-dependent description. In an inertial frame, there is no real outward force cancelling gravity: gravitational attraction remains the inward net force, while the spacecraft and crew move together along a curved trajectory.

The textbook also discusses microgravity rather than perfect zero gravity, including the practical challenges of floating, sleeping, eating and living for extended periods in a spacecraft.

Concept check: If gravity still acts on an astronaut in orbit, why does a weighing scale beneath their feet read nearly zero?

The scale reads the normal contact force, which becomes nearly zero when astronaut and scale are freely falling together. Earth's gravity continues to accelerate them toward Earth.

2. Artificial gravity in a rotating ring-shaped station

Imagine a person standing on the inside of the outer rim of a space station that spins at constant angular speed ω. The station floor pushes the person toward the rotation axis, providing the real centripetal force required to move in a circle.

The person experiences a weight-like sensation toward the outer rim. The textbook describes this as artificial gravity. In a frame rotating with the ring, one may represent the outward sensation by a centrifugal fictitious force; in an inertial frame, the floor's inward normal force causes the circular acceleration.

aartificial = v²/R = ω²R v = ωR    |    ω = 2π/T = 2πf

To simulate an Earth-like acceleration g = 9.8 m/s² at rim radius R, choose angular speed:

ω = √(g/R)    |    T = 2π√(R/g)    |    f = 1/T

The rotation rate in revolutions per minute (rpm) is rpm = 60f = 60/T. These ideal relations assume steady circular motion at the ring rim, without external gravity and without considering engineering or human comfort constraints.

Concept check: In the inertial frame, does an outward real force push the astronaut against the rim?

No. The actual floor contact force points inward and provides the required centripetal acceleration. The outward apparent force belongs to the rotating-frame description; it is not an extra real outward contact force.

3. Worked example — rotating space station

A ring-shaped station has radius R = 1500 m. Find the angular speed, time period and frequency needed to produce artificial acceleration g = 9.8 m/s².

  1. Angular speed: ω = √(g/R) = √(9.8/1500) ≈ 0.0808 rad/s (printed as ≈0.08 rad/s).
  2. Period: T = 2π√(R/g) = 2π√(1500/9.8) ≈ 77.73 s.
  3. Frequency: f = 1/T ≈ 0.0129 Hz (printed as ≈0.013 Hz).
  4. Equivalent rpm: rpm = 60/T ≈ 0.772 rpm.
Rounding matters. Using the rounded 0.08 rad/s to recompute the period creates a small discrepancy. Keep the unrounded angular speed for subsequent calculations, and round only the final answer.

4. Supplementary practice — spaceship rotating at 20 rpm

A cylindrical spaceship rotates at 20 revolutions per minute and has diameter 8 m. Calculate the artificial gravity at the outer wall.

  1. Radius = diameter/2 = 8/2 = 4 m.
  2. Angular speed ω = 20 × (2π/60) ≈ 2.094 rad/s.
  3. Artificial acceleration a = ω²R = (2.094)²(4) ≈ 17.55 m/s².
  4. Compared with g = 9.8 m/s², the sensation would be approximately 1.79 times Earth gravity.

This is a simplified mathematical classroom exercise, not a safe human-rated station design.
