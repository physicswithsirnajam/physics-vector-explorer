7.1 · Classification of solids · NBF pp. 113–116

How are solids classified?

Crystalline solids

Atoms, ions or molecules are arranged in a definite, regularly repeating three-dimensional pattern. A single crystal has continuous lattice order; some physical properties depend on direction.

Examples studied: sodium chloride, diamond and quartz.

Polycrystalline solids

These consist of many smaller ordered crystals called grains or crystallites. Each grain has an internal crystal structure, but neighboring grains can have different orientations.

Examples: many ordinary engineering metals.

Amorphous solids

Particles do not form a regular, long-range repeating crystal lattice. Local structural order can still exist. Many amorphous materials soften across a temperature range rather than showing a single sharp crystalline melting transition.

Examples: ordinary window glass and some polymers.

Compare their structures

FeatureCrystallinePolycrystallineAmorphous
Long-range orderRegular throughout one single crystalRegular within each grainNo overall repeating lattice
OrientationContinuous crystal orientationDifferent grains may point in different directionsNo single crystal orientation
ExampleSingle crystal of quartzTypical metal object made of many grainsWindow glass
Important distinction: Polycrystalline means many crystalline grains. Polymeric describes materials built from long chains of repeating units (monomers). These are different classifications: a polymer may be crystalline, partly crystalline or amorphous.

7.1 · Lattice and cell models

Crystal lattice and unit cell

A crystal lattice describes the regular pattern of equivalent positions throughout a crystal. A unit cell is a repeating geometric building block from which the lattice may be described.

Simple cubic (SC)

Its conventional cubic cell has lattice points at all eight corners.

Body-centred cubic (BCC)

In addition to the corner lattice points, a point occurs at the centre of the cube.

Face-centred cubic (FCC)

In addition to the corner points, a lattice point is at the centre of each of the six faces.

These are introductory descriptions of cubic unit-cell patterns, not diagrams drawn to atomic scale. Unit-cell packing calculations and other extended crystallography must be identified as supplementary unless verified in the required SLO.

Anisotropy

A crystalline material may show different values of a physical property in different directions because its particle arrangement depends on direction. This does not mean every property of every crystal must differ with direction.

X-ray diffraction

A diffraction pattern produced by X-rays interacting with regularly arranged crystal planes helps researchers investigate crystal structure.

Active recall · Teacher-prepared questions

Check your understanding

Answer each prompt before opening the model response. These are introductory revision prompts, not scored board-style MCQs or an official assessed test.

1. What structural feature distinguishes a crystalline solid from an amorphous solid?

A crystalline solid has a regular repeating long-range lattice. An amorphous solid has no overall long-range periodic lattice, although it may have local order.

2. Why is a polycrystalline solid not the same as an amorphous solid?

It consists of many small, internally ordered crystals (grains). An amorphous solid lacks those long-range ordered crystalline regions throughout the bulk.

3. How does a BCC unit cell differ from a simple cubic unit cell?

A BCC conventional cell contains an additional lattice point at its body centre; a simple cubic conventional cell has only the eight corner lattice points.

4. What is anisotropy?

It is the dependence of a measured physical property on direction. An ordered crystal may behave differently along different crystallographic directions.
