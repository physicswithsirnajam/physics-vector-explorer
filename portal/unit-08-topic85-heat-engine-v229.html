8.5 · What does a heat engine do?

A heat engine receives energy as heat from a hotter source, converts part of that energy to mechanical work, and rejects the remaining energy to a cooler sink. A heat engine working continuously in repeated cycles returns its working substance to the same initial thermodynamic state after each complete cycle.

Wnet,out = QH − QCQH = positive magnitude of heat absorbed; QC = positive magnitude of heat rejected; Wnet,out = net useful work delivered in one cycle

Because a cycle returns to its initial state, the net change in internal energy over a complete cycle is zero: ΔUcycle = 0. The First Law therefore gives the net-work relation above. For an operating engine producing positive work, QH must exceed QC.

Keep the signs clear: QH and QC are defined here as positive amounts. The heat actually leaving the working substance is −QC in the signed First Law. Thus, Qnet = QH − QC. Do not add two positive heat amounts as if both entered the engine.

Three parts and their roles

1 · Hot source

Provides energy to the engine through heat transfer at a relatively high temperature. Examples include a combustion source in a practical engine or an idealized hot reservoir.

2 · Cold sink

Receives rejected heat at a lower temperature. A continuously cycling heat engine transfers some energy away instead of converting all supplied heat into work.

3 · Working substance

A gas or vapour undergoes thermodynamic changes and transmits force to mechanical components. In a petrol engine, expanding combustion gases push the piston.

Steam and petrol engines are familiar examples, but they are not mechanically identical. Steam engines commonly transfer heat into a working fluid, whereas a petrol engine produces hot gases through combustion inside a cylinder.

A four-stroke petrol engine

The original textbook describes the suction, compression, power and exhaust strokes. In a conventional four-stroke spark-ignition engine, one complete working cycle takes four piston strokes, corresponding to two crankshaft revolutions.

StrokePiston movementWhat happens?
1 · Suction / IntakeDownFresh air–fuel charge enters through the intake valve.
2 · CompressionUpBoth valves are generally closed; the charge is compressed.
3 · PowerDownIgnition initiates combustion near the end of compression; expanding hot gases drive the piston down.
4 · ExhaustUpThe exhaust valve opens and the rising piston expels burnt gases.
Model distinction: The hot/cold reservoir flow is a general thermodynamic representation. The four-stroke petrol engine is an internal-combustion implementation, not a claim that its temperature or pressure is constant during a stroke. Four concept checks · Reveal the explanation

1. Why must an operating heat engine send some heat to a cold sink?
A continuously cyclic heat engine rejects some of the received thermal energy; it cannot convert every joule of supplied heat into net work. The detailed thermodynamic limit belongs to the later Second Law and Carnot lessons.
2. In one cycle, the engine absorbs 900 J and rejects 500 J. What net work does it deliver?
Net work = QH − QC = 900 J − 500 J = 400 J. The substance returns to its initial internal energy after a complete cycle.
3. Which stroke supplies the main outward mechanical push in a four-stroke petrol engine?
The power stroke: hot combustion gases expand and push the piston downward.
4. Does QC = 300 J mean +300 J enters the working substance?
No. QC is defined as a positive amount rejected; in the signed First Law, that heat transfer is −300 J for the working substance.
