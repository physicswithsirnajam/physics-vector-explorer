## Scientific correctness and teaching cautions

- **Reversible:** ideal-limit path that can be retraced, returning **system and surroundings** to their initial conditions with no net change anywhere. Near-equilibrium/quasi-static progression and absence of dissipative effects are required in the idealization. **Slow or quasi-static alone is NOT sufficient**; friction can make even a very slow change irreversible.
- **Irreversible:** friction/dissipation, unrestrained expansion, and spontaneous heat transfer across a **finite temperature difference** are standard examples. Applying external work can return the system to a previous state while leaving environmental changes, so this is not the reverse path of a reversible process.
- **Phase changes:** the source includes freezing, melting, boiling, condensation and evaporation as reversible *examples*. Distinguish ideal equilibrium changes induced by arbitrarily small driving differences from everyday boiling/evaporation involving finite thermal gradients. Do **not** claim all real melting or boiling is reversible.
- **Cyclic process:** a thermodynamic system returns to the same full initial state after successive changes; state variable U then satisfies **ΔU_cycle=0**. With the same sign convention as source Topic8.4, **Q_net,cycle = W_by,net,cycle**; neither net heat nor net work necessarily equals zero. A cycle need not be reversible — the surrounding environment may suffer frictional or heat-transfer losses. Original Figure8.15 is described, not embedded or redrawn.
- **Do not conflate:** *cyclic* refers to return of the system state, *reversible* additionally refers to a possible exact restoring of system and surroundings. The next formal Second Law discussion is not claimed to have been taught or approved in this preview.

## Original six-scenario unscored explainer

Each is student-facing explanatory practice, not a publisher exercise or scored assessment:
1. Frictionless near-equilibrium piston, tiny mechanical pressure differences — reversible **in the ideal limit** assuming no additional dissipative heat paths.
2. Very slow piston **with friction** — irreversible.
3. Hot object contacting a distinctly cooler object (finite ΔT) — irreversible.
4. Equilibrium phase change with arbitrarily small thermal differences — reversible **in the ideal limit**.
5. Gas freely expanding into vacuum — irreversible.
6. Engine fluid returning to its original state after a frictional operating cycle — **cyclic, but irreversible**.
