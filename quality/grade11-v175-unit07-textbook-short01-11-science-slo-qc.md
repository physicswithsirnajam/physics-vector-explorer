## Only questions requiring attention — amendments, reasons and original-retention decisions

| Source Q | Priority | Independent physics/answer-quality finding | Can the original remain? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **7.1** | **Optional** | Many crystalline arrangements are thermodynamically favorable under suitable conditions, but **not all solids always crystallize**; glasses may form because structural rearrangement is kinetically slow. A glass transition involves a change in glassy/viscous behavior over a temperature range and is **not the same as sharply defined crystal melting**. | **Yes.** Keep both parts of the original. The teacher's model answer should state *favorable free energy plus kinetic constraints*, avoiding an absolute rule. |
| **7.2** | **Compulsory** | Some old window glasses become hazy through **surface weathering, moisture/chemical attack, leaching or deposited films**. This is **not caused by old window glass noticeably flowing downward at room temperature**, nor does every old window develop milky opacity. Historic surface deterioration is well documented; the source question should not teach the flowing-glass myth. | **Yes.** Retain original; approve a scientifically corrected *conditional* model answer and acknowledge other causes of cloudiness. |
| **7.3** | **Compulsory** | Under an externally applied load, **stress** can be described as the imposed mechanical condition and **strain** as the resulting deformation. But the wording “more fundamental” has **no universal constitutive answer**: displacement/strain can itself be prescribed, with stress developing, and thermal eigenstrain can occur. | **Yes.** Preserve question; accept a context-specific stress-causes-strain explanation **only with an explicit external-load assumption**, or use a teacher-reviewed explanatory alternative that compares cause/effect without declaring an absolute ranking. |
| **7.4** | **Optional** | Uniaxial tension can cause a **volume change**, because the body extends axially and ordinarily contracts laterally; the net volume change depends on its material response. For nearly incompressible response the change may be small. A small-strain Poisson ratio formula is an *enrichment*, not required by the named source textbook short-response objective. | **Yes.** Keep original, explain longitudinal versus lateral strain qualitatively, and do not penalize answers noting the incompressible limit. |
| **7.5** | **Compulsory** | For **force vertical, extension horizontal**, the graph gradient is **stiffness** `dF/dx`, reducing to constant `k` in the straight proportional regime. **NBF Fig.7.14 reverses the axes**, in which case the graph gradient is **compliance** `dx/dF`. Without specified axes and interval, the expected explanation is ambiguous. Also the named B-27 Application outcome concerns **work by area**, not simply a slope definition. | **Yes.** Keep original; the model answer or corrected standalone candidate **must define the graph axes and its linearity assumptions**. Teach slope distinct from area. |
| **7.6** | **Compulsory** | The **recoverable strain energy stored** in a stable elastic body is **nonnegative relative to an undeformed zero-energy reference**. By contrast, the **change** of stored energy can be negative during unloading; external work in unloading can have an opposite sign. These are three different quantities, so avoid a simplistic universal yes/no without a reference. | **Yes.** Preserve original; use an approved rubric distinguishing stored energy from negative energy change or signed work, with loading/unloading examples. |
| **7.7** | **Optional** | Elastic strain energy depends on **loading magnitude and path, material stiffness, geometry and the load/deformation mode** (axial tension/compression, shear, bending, torsion). Merely listing load names is incomplete; shear and torsion formulae may be beyond the precise B-28 summative demand. | **Yes.** Keep original for broad learning; give qualitative examples and restrict scored B-28 evidence to its actual proportional force–extension energy outcome. |
| **7.8** | **Compulsory** | Bridges can have a nominal **design or intended service life**, but the passing of an arbitrary date does **not automatically make a bridge unsafe**. Fatigue under traffic cycles, corrosion, damage, professional inspection, load rating, maintenance, rehabilitation and remaining capacity govern continued safe use. **Safety can also fail before design life**, so neither “always dangerous” nor “always safe” is defensible. | **Yes.** Retain the real-world context; correct the model answer to condition-based assessment and clarify that qualified engineers and authorities decide actual bridge serviceability. |
| **7.9** | **Optional** | Repeated bending can produce **cyclic localized plastic strain and fatigue damage**, initiate and propagate cracks, and eventually fracture wire. Material work-hardening may influence the process but is **not the sole universal mechanism**. The fatigue-life application is broader than simply describing elastic/plastic limit under B-26. | **Yes.** Preserve the original as supplementary application; provide a scientifically nuanced fatigue explanation rather than promising a specific number of bends or an always-identical fracture mechanism. |
| **7.10** | **Optional** | The elastic modulus indicates **stiffness**, the ratio of stress to strain in a suitable linear elastic regime. A high Young modulus does **not by itself establish ultimate tensile strength, toughness or ductility**. The source short-response reasoning is mostly Understanding despite official B-24 being an Application SLO. | **Yes.** Keep original and explicitly distinguish stiffness from strength in the teacher explanation. |

**No original-content amendment identified for Q7.11** in the first pass. The expected explanation is a **regular, periodically repeating array of lattice points** defining equivalent sites and translational symmetry; for a crystal with multiple atoms per repeating unit, the **basis/motif** is associated with lattice sites. A crystal's Bravais lattice is not simply a list of all individual atoms. This is a source-valid fundamental definition, but teacher approval is still pending.
