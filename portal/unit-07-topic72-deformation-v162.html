7.2 · Printed p. 116

What is deformation?

When a force changes a solid's dimensions or shape, that change is called deformation. Pulling a spring can lengthen it; squeezing a ball changes its shape. An object may recover its original dimensions when the force is removed or retain a permanent change.

Elastic deformation

Reversible shape or size change. On unloading, the object returns to its original condition while it remains within its elastic range.

Plastic deformation

Permanent change after loading has passed the elastic limit. Removing the force does not completely restore the original shape.

Textbook connection: NBF Figure 7.7 uses a squeezed ball and crystal slip/twinning to introduce changes under applied forces.

7.2.1 · Printed pp. 117–118

Stress and strain: not the same quantity

Stress, σ

Force per unit area carrying that force. It tells us how strongly a body is loaded.

σ = F / AUnit: N m−2 = Pa · Dimensions: M L−1 T−2

Strain, ε

The fractional change in a dimension. It measures how much a body deforms relative to its original size.

ε = ΔL / LTensile strain: dimensionless; no SI unit

Three corresponding deformation modes

ModeStressStrainExample
Tensile / compressiveNormal force divided by cross-section, F/AΔL/L (signed or magnitude, as specified)Stretching or compressing a rod
ShearTangential force per area, F/AΔx/h for a sideways displacement Δx over face separation hTop face of a block sliding relative to its bottom
Volume / hydrostaticUniform pressure changeΔV/V; negative under compression if the sign is retainedCompressing a solid uniformly
Exam distinction: Stress has the units of pressure (Pa). Strain is a ratio of like quantities and therefore has no units. Do not substitute a length change in millimetres directly into a formula that expects metres.

7.2.2 · Printed pp. 118–119

Modulus of elasticity

For a material behaving in its linear elastic range, the appropriate modulus is the ratio of stress to the corresponding strain. Since strain is dimensionless, an elastic modulus has SI unit pascal (Pa). The three moduli describe different loading modes.

FBISE scope: P-11-B-24 explicitly names stress, strain and Young’s modulus for summative Application. Bulk and shear moduli are also included in the NBF textbook and are taught here for broader chapter understanding; this page does not independently certify them as separate summative outcomes.

Young’s modulus, Y

Resistance to axial stretching or compression, related to tensile/compressive strain.

Y = (F/A) / (ΔL/L)Equivalent: Y = FL / (AΔL)

NBF equation 7.1; Figure 7.9.

Bulk modulus, B

Resistance to fractional volume change under uniform pressure.

B = −Δp / (ΔV/V)The minus sign makes B positive for ordinary compression.

NBF equation 7.2; Figure 7.10. Here Δp denotes the pressure increase.

Shear modulus, G

Resistance to change of shape under tangential loading.

G = (F/A) / (Δx/h)For small angles: shear strain ≈ θ in radians.

NBF equations 7.3–7.4; Figure 7.11. The textbook uses S for shear modulus.

Physics precision: The textbook introduces elastic modulus as a constant. That is the approximation for the linear elastic region; a material need not obey a constant stress/strain ratio after yielding or outside its proportional range.

Reading a stress–strain curve

The horizontal axis represents strain, while the vertical axis represents stress. This original schematic gives a conceptual shape and is not drawn to scale; exact positions vary by material.

Point / regionWhat it means in the textbook’s model
O → AApproximately straight-line, proportional stress and strain; Hooke’s law applies.
AProportional limit: linearity ends. This is not necessarily the elastic limit.
BElastic limit / upper-yield region as labelled in the textbook. Up to this approximate boundary, unloading can restore original shape.
CLower yield region, indicating the start of substantial plastic deformation.
DUltimate tensile strength: maximum engineering stress on this typical ductile curve.
EFracture: the material breaks after sufficient deformation.

Ductile materials can stretch plastically before breaking; brittle materials typically fracture with much less plastic deformation. Do not assume that a schematic applies identically to all substances.

Quick self-check

Answer each question yourself first, then expand the explanation. These are original concept prompts, not official board MCQs.

1. Why does tensile strain have no unit?

It is the ratio of two lengths, ΔL/L, so the units cancel.

2. Which modulus is associated with uniform compression from all sides?

Bulk modulus. It compares the pressure change with fractional volume change.

3. How is shear strain obtained from a small lateral displacement?

Divide the sideways displacement by the separation of the faces: εshear = Δx/h (approximately the shear angle in radians for small angles).

4. Is the proportional limit always identical to the elastic limit?

No. A material can stop obeying a straight-line stress–strain relation yet still recover when the load is removed, over some additional range.

5. What is the difference between points D and E on the typical ductile-material curve?

D represents the maximum engineering stress (ultimate tensile strength), whereas E is the later fracture or breaking point.
