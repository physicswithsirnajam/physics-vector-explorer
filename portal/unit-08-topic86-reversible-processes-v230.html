Reversible and Irreversible Processes

Can a thermodynamic change be undone so that both the system and its surroundings return to their original states? The distinction explains why ideal thermodynamic paths are useful models, while real processes often cannot be exactly reversed.

8.6 · What makes a process reversible?

Reversible process · Ideal limit

A process is thermodynamically reversible if it can be retraced in reverse while restoring both the system and the surroundings to their original states, with no net change left anywhere.

In the ideal limit, the system passes through states arbitrarily close to equilibrium, and tiny changes of an external condition can reverse the direction without dissipative losses.

Source: original NBF printed p.141, concept of retracing a process without leaving changes to its surroundings.

Irreversible process · Real-world tendency

An irreversible process cannot be exactly retraced so that the system and environment are both restored with no other changes. Friction, unrestrained expansion, mixing and heat transfer across a finite temperature difference are common sources of irreversibility.

Some parts of a system may return to their earlier conditions, but this is not enough to establish reversibility.

Source: original NBF printed p.142, dissipative phenomena and examples.

Key distinction: A process being slow or quasi-static is not sufficient for reversibility. A very slow piston moving with friction still dissipates energy and is irreversible. Reversibility requires absence of dissipative effects and appropriately infinitesimal driving differences.

Examples: physical interpretation matters

Idealized near-equilibrium phase change

Melting and freezing, or evaporation and condensation, can approximate reversible paths if the material and thermal surroundings remain in equilibrium and the change is driven by arbitrarily small temperature differences.

Do not generalize: ordinary boiling with a large heater–liquid temperature difference or rapid evaporation is not automatically reversible.

Friction or dissipative motion

Friction converts organized mechanical energy into thermal energy. It is not possible to reverse the motion alone and recover the original conditions of both the body and its surroundings without other changes.

Heat flow across a finite difference

When a hot body is brought into contact with a substantially colder one, heat flows spontaneously to the cooler body. A naive reversal of that flow cannot restore everything without additional changes elsewhere.

Textbook qualification: The source mentions phase changes as reversible examples. These are understood as idealized, near-equilibrium cases, not a blanket claim that every real melting or boiling event is fully reversible.

Cyclic process ≠ reversible process

The original textbook also introduces a cyclic process: after a sequence of changes, the system returns to its initial thermodynamic state. For example, it may follow one path from state 1 to state 2 and another path from state 2 back to state 1.

State 1Initial system state
Forward 1 → 2 · Return 2 → 1
State 2Intermediate system state
ΔUcycle = 0 After a complete cycle: Qnet,cycle = Wby,net,cycle using signed heat and work.

Internal energy is a state function. Its net change over a complete cycle is zero, even when heat and work cross the system boundary along the path. Net heat and net work need not individually be zero.

Do not confuse these ideas: A cycle requires the system to return to its starting state. Reversibility additionally requires the surroundings to be restorable with no net change. A real heat-engine cycle with friction is cyclic but irreversible.

Reversible in the ideal limit

A frictionless piston kept near mechanical equilibrium and moved by arbitrarily small changes of external pressure can, in the ideal limit, retrace its path without losses. Heat transfer, if any, must also avoid finite dissipative gradients.

Five self-checks · Reveal answers on demand

1. If a piston moves extremely slowly, is its motion necessarily reversible?
No. Friction or other dissipative effects can remain even at very low speed. Slow motion by itself does not establish reversibility.
2. What must be restored to prove thermodynamic reversibility?
Both the system and its surroundings must be restorable to their original states with no net changes remaining.
3. Can a gas return to its initial state after an irreversible process?
Yes. A complete cycle can return the gas to its initial state even though its surroundings have undergone irreversible changes. Cyclic and reversible are not synonyms.
4. In a complete cycle, if the net heat transferred to the system is +400 J, what net work has it done?
The system returns to the same internal energy, so ΔUcycle = 0 and Qnet = Wby,net = +400 J. This does not prove the cycle reversible.
5. Are melting and boiling automatically reversible as they happen in daily life?
No. These processes can approximate reversibility in an ideal near-equilibrium limit with infinitesimal driving gradients. Finite temperature differences, mixing or rapid events make ordinary real processes irreversible.
