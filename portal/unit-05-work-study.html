Section 5.1 · NBF pp. 78–80 · supporting concept + P-11-B-30

1. Work and the force–displacement graph

Work is the scalar product of a force and the object's displacement. The angle must be measured between the force and displacement vectors. A force perpendicular to displacement does no mechanical work along that displacement.

W = Fd cos θ   ·   1 J = 1 N·m
  • Positive: the force has a forward component, with θ less than 90°.
  • Zero: the force is perpendicular (θ = 90°).
  • Negative: the component opposes displacement (θ greater than 90°).

For a variable force, plot the component along the displacement, F∥, versus displacement d. The signed area between this graph and the d-axis is work. Regions above the axis add positive work; regions below subtract work.

W = signed area under F∥–d graph   ≈ ΣF∥,iΔdi

For a force that varies linearly from F0 to F1 over displacement d, the average component is (F0 + F1)/2, so:

W = ½(F0 + F1)d

Worked example based on NBF Example 5.1 (p.80): A suitcase is pulled with 16.8 N at 30° over 4.90 m. Pulling-force work = 16.8 × 4.90 × cos30° ≈ 71.3 J. If friction of 0.170 N opposes motion, friction work = −0.170 × 4.90 ≈ −0.833 J. Net work ≈ 70.5 J, with weight and normal reaction perpendicular to the horizontal displacement.

Section 5.2 · NBF p. 81 · P-11-B-31

2. Conservative and non-conservative forces

The work done by a conservative force between two points does not depend on the path. A conservative force performs zero total work around a closed path. Gravitational work near Earth's surface is Wg = mg(hstart − hend), regardless of route.

A non-conservative force such as kinetic friction or drag generally does path-dependent work and converts some mechanical energy to internal/thermal energy. For an idealised constant resistive force Fr opposing motion over a path length L, Wr,by = −FrL.

ComparisonConservativeNon-conservative
Path between endpointsWork independent of pathWork may depend on path length and route
Closed pathNet work is zeroGenerally not zero
ExamplesGravity, ideal spring force, electrostatic forceKinetic friction, viscous drag
Energy pictureReversible transfer between kinetic/potential energyMechanical energy can turn into internal energy
Section 5.3 · NBF pp. 81–82 · P-11-B-29

3. Deriving kinetic energy (no skipped steps)

  1. Assume constant net force along a straight displacement d, moving mass m from speed u to speed v.
  2. Newton's second law: Fnet = ma.
  3. Equation of motion: v² − u² = 2ad.
  4. Net work: Wnet = Fnetd = mad.
  5. Since ad = (v² − u²)/2, Wnet = ½m(v² − u²).
  6. Therefore Wnet = ½mv² − ½mu² = Kfinal − Kinitial.
  7. If the body starts from rest (u = 0), the kinetic energy at speed v is:
K = ½mv²   (J)

At a fixed speed, doubling mass doubles K. At a fixed mass, doubling speed quadruples K. K is nonnegative for an ordinary body with positive mass.

Section 5.4 & 5.4.1 · NBF pp. 82–83 · P-11-B-32

4. Work–energy theorem in a resistive medium

Wnet = ΔK = ½m(v² − u²)

If a forward applied force Fa acts over displacement d and a constant resistive force Fr opposes motion, net work is (Fa − Fr)d. This equals the change in kinetic energy.

Wapplied = ΔK + Wagainst resistance   where   Wagainst resistance = Frd
Example: A 5 kg object initially moving at 2 m/s travels 4 m under a 30 N applied force opposed by a 12 N constant resistive force. K0 = ½(5)(2²) = 10 J; Wa = 120 J; Wr,by = −48 J; Wnet = 72 J; Kf = 82 J; v = √(2 × 82 / 5) ≈ 5.73 m/s.

Physical reachability: A calculation that predicts negative final kinetic energy means an object cannot reach the chosen distance under these assumed constant opposing forces. It stops earlier; do not take the square root of a negative energy or silently report zero as though it arrived at the target.

Supporting extension, Section 5.4.2 pp.83–84: machine efficiency = useful energy output / total energy input × 100%. This is contextual teaching, not a fifth invented summative SLO.
